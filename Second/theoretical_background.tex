\section{Linear Algebra}


For this section, we will specifically set up the euklidian (Hilbert) space $(\R^{N_h}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G)$,
with the inner product
\begin{equation*}
    \langle u, v \rangle_\G := u^T \G v,
\end{equation*}
for some symmetric positive definite $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ for each $u, v \in \R^{N_h}$ and
$\norm{\cdot}$ its induced norm. 
As preparation for the upcoming Bauer Fike Theorem, we further want to define the matrix norm for the 
matrix space $\R^{N_h \times N}$,
where $N \leq N_h$, as follows
\begin{equation*}
    \opnorm{A} := \sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(A^T \G A)},
\end{equation*}
for $\lambda_\text{max}(A^T \G A)$ being the maximal spectral radius of $A^T \G A$, 
which exists because $A^T \G A$ is symmetric and positive semi definite
and hence subject to the spectral theorem.
This norm satifies the wanted property mentioned in the original paper \cite[p. 137-141]{bauer1960la}; any operator norm
such that
\begin{equation*}
    \norm{Ax}^2 = x^T A^T \G A x \leq \lambda_\text{max}(A^T \G A) x^T x \leq \opnorm{A}^2 \euknorm{x}^2
\end{equation*}
for all $x \in \R^{N}$. 

\begin{theorem}[Bauer-Fike Theorem] \label{theo: bauer fike}
    Let $K \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be some diagonalizable matrices with $\sigma_k$ eigenvalues and 
    $V = (\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N) \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be the stacked eigenvectors matrix of $K$ 
    and $\delta K \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ some symmetric matrix. Then if $\lambda_{k}$ are the eigenvalues of 
    $K + \delta K$, we have 
    \begin{equation*}
        \min\limits_{k} \vert \lambda_k - \sigma_k \vert \leq \kappa(V) \opnorm{(K + \delta K) - K},
    \end{equation*}
    where $\kappa(V) = \opnorm{V} \opnorm{V^{-1}}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[p. 137-141]{bauer1960la}.
\end{proof}

Furthermore, we will often discuss the notion of orthonormality with regard to a a matrix. This
can be understood with the following definition:

\begin{definition}[Matrix Orthonormality] \label{def: matrix orthonormality}
    Let $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be a symmetric positive definit matrix for some $N_h \in \N$. We then say that
    any matrix $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ is $\G$-orthonormal for some $N \leq N_h$, if
    \begin{equation*}
        \I = \A^T \G \A.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    If some matrix $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ can be written as $\V = \A \tilde{\V}$, where
    $\tilde{\V} \in \R^{N_A \times N}$ and $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ being a $\G$-orthonormal matrix, 
    then $\V$ is $\G$-orthonormal if and only if $\tilde{\V}$ is orthonormal in the euklidian sense. 
    In fact,
    \begin{equation*}
        \V^T \G \V = \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G \A \tilde{\V} = \tilde{\V}^T \tilde{\V}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Orthogonal Projector] \label{def: orthogonal projector}
    A operator $P: V \to V$, where $V$ is a Hilbert space with inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_V$,
    is called an \emph{orthogonal projector} onto $W \subset V$, if
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $P^2 = P$,
        \item $\text{Im}(P) = W$ and
        \item $\langle Px, y \rangle_V = \langle x, Py \rangle_V$ for all $x, y \in V$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: orthogonal projector on span of A}
    Let $(\R^{N_h}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G)$ be the Hilbert space from above. If now
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ is a $\G$-orthonormal matrix, then
    \begin{equation*}
        P := \A \A^T \G
    \end{equation*}
    is a orthogonal projector on $\text{span}(\A)$ with regard to the $\G$ induced inner product.
    We refer to this as $P$ being the \emph{$\G$-orthonormal projector} on $\text{span}(\A)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    It suffices to simply check the items of Definition (\ref{def: orthogonal projector}); the image
    statement is instantaneously clear, since $\A \A^T \G = \A (\A^T \G)$. The idempotency can be
    checked via
    \begin{equation*}
        P^2 = (\A \A^T \G)(\A \A^T \G) = \A (\A^T \G \A) \A^T \G = \A \A^T \G = P.
    \end{equation*}
    Finally set $u, v \in \R^{N_h}$ arbitrarily, then 
    \begin{align*}
        \langle P u, v \rangle_\G &= (P u)^T \G v \\
        &= u^T P^T \G v \\
        &= u^T (\A \A^T \G)^T v \\
        &= u^T \G \A \A^T \G v \\
        &= u^T \G P v = \langle u, Pv \rangle_\G.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: opnorm A}
    Let $(\R^{N_h}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G)$ be the Hilbert space from above.
    If now
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ is a $\G$-orthonormal matrix, then
    \begin{equation*}
        \opnorm{\A} = 1.
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This is directly clear, since $\opnorm{\A} = \sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(\A^T \G \A)} =
    \sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(\I)} = 1$.
\end{proof}


These preliminaries will play an important role, since we will use such a 
gramian $\G$ of an a priori chosen basis under a discretization of a PDE to set up a (linear) projector
attaining any projection onto the solution manifold under the span of a $\G$-orthonormal matrix.
In fact, any projector 
\begin{equation*}
    \V \V^T \G : \R^{N_h} \to \R^N
\end{equation*}
for a $\G$-orthonormal matrix $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ and some $N \leq N_h$ can be seen as a special
case for the differential geometric framework 
of (nonlinear) projectors on manifolds concerning Reduced Order Modelling introduced
and discussed in \textcite{buchfink2023}.

\subsection{Sobolev Spaces}




For our work with Partial Differential Equation, and subsequent error approximations, we want to introduce
Sobolev spaces. These are generalized differentiable spaces, where a weak derivative is given, as an analogon to the
classic derivative in the $C^n(\Omega)$ 
space. They are employed in order to exploit their compact embedding in the Hölder spaces, which are generalizations
of $C^n(\Omega)$. We will not go in much detail about the theory of Sobolev spaces, since we mostly concern ourselves
with finite dimensional subspaces, but some ground truth must be discussed. 

For this subsection we primary use \cite{alt2016}, and for the first upcoming definition \cite{dacorogna2004}.

\begin{definition}[Lipschitz Domain]
    Let $d \in \N$, and $\Omega \subset \R^d$. Then $\Omega$ is called a \emph{Lipschitz domain} if for every
    $x \in \partial \Omega$ and some $r > 0$, there is $U := B_{r, \vert \cdot \vert}(x) \subset \R^d$ and an isomorphic
    map $H: Q \to B_{r, \vert \cdot \vert}(x)$ with $Q := \{ x \in \R^d \, \vert \, \norm{x}_{\ell^1} < d \}$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
        H \in C^k(\bar{Q}), \quad H^{-1} \in C^k(\bar{U}), \quad 
        H(Q_+) = U \cap \Omega, \quad H(Q_0) = U \cap \partial \Omega
    \end{equation*}
    where $Q_+ := \{x \in Q \, \vert \, x_d > 0\}$ and similarily $Q_0 := \{x \in Q \, \vert \, x_d = 0 \}$,
    and $H$ is only in $C^{0, 1}$.
\end{definition}

From now on, let $d \in \N$ and, if not given otherwise, $\Omega \subset \R^d$ be a Lipschitz domain.

\begin{definition}[Weak Derivative]
    Let $\alpha \in \N_0^d$ be a multiindex. A function $u \in L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)$ is said to have an \emph{$\alpha$-th 
    weak derivative}, if there is a function $v_\alpha \in L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \int_\Omega u D^\alpha \varphi = (-1)^{\vert \alpha \vert} \int_\Omega v_\alpha \varphi \quad \forall
        \varphi \in C_0^\infty(\Omega).
    \end{equation*}
    We then write $v_\alpha = D^\alpha u$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sobolev Space] \label{def: sobolev space}
    Let $n \in \N_0$ and $1 \leq p \leq \infty$. Then we define the \emph{Sobolev space} as
    \begin{equation*}
        W^{n, p}(\Omega) := \{ f \in L^p(\Omega) \, \vert \, \norm{f}_{W^{n, p}(\Omega)} < \infty \},
    \end{equation*}
    where the norm on this space is given via
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{f}_{W^{n, p}(\Omega)} := \begin{cases}
            \displaystyle
            \left( \sum\limits_{\vert \alpha \vert \leq n} \norm{D^\alpha f}^p_{L^p(\Omega)} \right)^{1/p}, 
            \quad &\text{if } p<\infty \\[1.5em]
            \displaystyle
            \, \max\limits_{\vert \alpha \vert \leq n} \, \norm{D^\alpha f}_{L^\infty(\Omega)}, \quad &\text{if } p = \infty,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    for any $f \in W^{n, p}(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

It is possible to extend this definition for $0 < n < 1$ to that of the \emph{Sobolev-Slobodeckij spaces}. In the 
analysis of partial differential equations these are used to characterize the regularity of Sobolev functions,
which are restricted to the boundary $\partial \Omega$. This restriction itself is called a trace operator.
One of the statements of relevance for such fractal Sobolev spaces is the following:

If $\Omega \subset \R^d$ is a regular open set (for example, if $\Omega$ is a Lipschitz domain), and if 
$u \in W^{1, p}(\Omega)$, then in fact $u \mapsto u \vert_{\partial \Omega}$ as a trace operator is surjective
from $W^{1, p}(\Omega)$ onto $W^{1-(1/p), p}(\partial \Omega)$ and
\begin{equation*}
    \norm{u \vert_{\partial \Omega}}_{W^{1-(1/p), p}(\partial \Omega)} \leq C \norm{u}_{W^{1, p}(\Omega)},
\end{equation*}
for some $C > 0$ independent of $p$ and $u$, see \cite[p. 315]{brezis2011}.
This of course only makes sense in light of the next definition.

\begin{definition}[Sobolev-Slobodeckij Space]
    Let $0 < s < 1$ and $1 \leq p \leq \infty$. Then we define the \emph{Sobolev-Slobodeckij space} as
    \begin{equation*}
        W^{s, p}(\Omega) := \{ f \in L^p(\Omega) \, \vert \, \norm{f}_{W^{s, p}(\Omega)} < \infty \},
    \end{equation*}
    where the norm on this space is given via
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{f}_{W^{s, p}(\Omega)} := \begin{cases}
            \displaystyle
            \left( \norm{f}_{L^p(\Omega)}^p + \int_\Omega \int_\Omega 
            \left( \frac{\vert f(x) - f(y) \vert}{\vert x-y \vert^{s + d/p}}\right)^p dx dy \right)^{1/p}, 
            \quad &\text{if } p<\infty \\[1.5em]
            \displaystyle
            \, \max \left\{\norm{f}_{L^\infty(\Omega)}, \operatorname{ess} \!\! \sup_{x, y \in \Omega} 
            \frac{\vert f(x)-f(y) \vert}{\vert x-y \vert^{s}} \right\}, \quad &\text{if } p = \infty,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    for any $f \in W^{s, p}(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

Furthermore, a very important theorem result will be the following theorem, translating Lipschitz continuity into
Sobolev-regularity. This will allow us to employ the later introduced approximation theory for neural networks
from the sole position of Lipschitz continuity.

\begin{theorem} \label{theo: lipschitz implies sobolev}
    Let $f: \Omega \to \R$ be a bounded and $L$-Lipschitz map, then $f \in W^{1, \infty}(\Omega)$, the 
    weak gradient of $f$ agrees almost everywhere with the classical gradient, and
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{D f(x)}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)} \leq L.
    \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[Theorem 4.1]{heinonen2005} and \cite[Remark 4.2]{heinonen2005}.
\end{proof}




\subsection{Reduced Basis Framework}  \label{subsec: reduced basis framework}


For this subsection we define $u(\mu_j, t_k) \in \R^{N_h}$ for $(\mu_j)_{j = 1}^{N_s} =: \Pcal \subset \Theta$ and 
$(t_k)_{k = 1}^{N_t} =: \Tcal \subset [0, T]$ to be some vectors, later representing the solutions to a 
parametric PDE for a 
given parameter and time point in a given discrete subspace of the coefficient solution manifold. 
Here $\Theta$ represents and arbitrary parameter space, 
$T > 0$ some arbitrary time point, and $N_s, N_t \in \N$ be the parameter and time sample size respectively. 
Let the inner product on $\R^{N_h}$ be defined here, 
using a symmetric positive definite matrix $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ via 
\begin{equation*}
    \langle u, v \rangle_\G := u^T \G v, \qquad \text{for all } u, v \in \R^{N_h},
\end{equation*}
and the corresponding norm $\norm{\cdot} := \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G$.
This is meant to result from a choice of an inner product stemming from the coefficient vectors $u(\mu, t)$ 
representing that space, in other words, given by the choice of a basis
on the corresponding Hilbert space, when viewing $u(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h}$ as the coefficients 
identifying an element in
that space. 
It is assumed,
that we sample the parameters and time points uniformly and iid from their respective spaces and that 
$\norm{u(\mu, t)} < \infty$ for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $t \in [0, T]$ in an essential
sense. Let also 
\begin{equation*}
    N_\text{data} := N_s \cdot N_t,
\end{equation*}
be the total sample size.

Note, that, with the discussion surrounding the Definition (\ref{def: matrix orthonormality}) of the
$\G$-orthonormality, taking the error of any such gram-sensitive projector will result in the 
possibility of switching to a low dimensional euklidian norm, if the matrix $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ 
for some $N \leq N_h$ is
$\G$-orthonormal and can be expressed as $\V = \A \tilde{\V}$, where $\A \in \R^{N_A \times N}$
for $N \leq N_A \leq N_h$ is also $\G$-orthonormal. This is quantified by the following result:

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: switch to euklidian norm}
    Let $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ 
    for some $N \leq N_h$ be
    $\G$-orthonormal and be written as 
    \begin{equation*}
        \V = \A \tilde{\V},
    \end{equation*}
    where $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$
    is also $\G$-orthonormal and $\tilde{\V} \in \R^{N_A \times N}$ for $N \leq N_A \leq N_h$. 
    Then we have, for any 
    $u \in \R^{N_h}$, that
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{u - \V \V^T \G u}^2 = c_\A + \euknorm{u_\text{pre-red} - \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T u_\text{pre-red}}^2,
    \end{equation*}
    with $c_\A > 0$ being a constant only depending on $\A$ and $u_\text{pre-red} = \A^T \G u$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    By Lemma (\ref{lemma: orthogonal projector on span of A}), we know that $\A \A^T \G$ is a $\G$-orthonormal 
    projector into the span of $\A$, and thus its residual $u - \A \A^T \G u$ must be 
    $\G$-orthogonal to all columns of $\A$, since we can decompose 
    $u = u_\perp + u_\parallel$, where $u_\parallel \in \text{span}(\A)$ and 
    $u_\perp \in \R^{N_h} - \text{span}(\A)$. This gives us the possibility to use the Pythagoras decomposition:
    \begin{align*}
        \norm{u - \V \V^T \G u}^2 &= \norm{u - \A \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G u}^2 \\
        &= \norm{u - \A \A^T \G u}^2 + \norm{\A \A^T \G u - \A \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G u}^2 \\
        &= \norm{u - \A \A^T \G u}^2 + \euknorm{\A^T \G u - \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G u}^2,
    \end{align*}
    where the last step is justified by showing that for any $u \in \R^{N_A}$
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{\A u} = \langle \A u, \A u \rangle_\G = (\A u)^T \G \A u = u^T \A^T \G \A u = u^T u = \euknorm{u}.
    \end{equation*}
    Now setting $u_\text{pre-red} := \A^T \G u$ gives us the wanted statement.
\end{proof}

This proposition might seem quite unimportant, but this will be the core idea giving us a possibility to
assert closeness with regard to the representations of the solution rather than the, potentially uninformative,
solution coefficient vectors in $\R^{N_h}$ under a computationally feasible norm (the euklidian $N_A$-dimensional
norm).

Next, we will discuss prerequisites to the Proper Orthogonal Decomposition and related concepts, such as the 
Kolmogorov $n$-width, which lets us derive many key concepts and is an important quantity in the field of ROM methods. 
We base this subsection on \cite{quarteroni2015rb}, but import the generalizations to arbitrary inner products from
\cite{franco2024}. 
\begin{definition}[Generalized Proper Orthogonal Decomposition] \label{def: POD}
    Assuming $N_\text{data} < \infty$ and let $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be a mass matrix, 
    we define by $U_j = [u(\mu_j, t_k)]_{k = 1}^{N_t} \in \R^{N_h \times N_t}$ 
    the snapshot matrix for a given $\mu_j \in \Pcal$ and consequently $U = [U_j]_{j=1}^{N_s}
    \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}}$ 
    the collective snapshot matrix. Then the \emph{(discrete) correlation matrix} is given by
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: discrete correlation matrix}
        K = \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} U \G U^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}.
    \end{equation}
    $K$ is symmetric and semi-positive definite and thus has real and 
    positive eigenvalues $\sigma^2 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r^2$. 
    We further call $\psi^\mu_k$ the eigenvectors of the correlation matrix $K$. 
    With these, the corresponding POD basis of dimension $N < N_h$ is given via
    \begin{equation*}
        \xi_k = \frac{1}{\sigma_k} U \psi_k, \quad 1 \leq k \leq N,
    \end{equation*}
    for the $N$ largest eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors. Then stacking these eigenvectors gives us
    the \emph{POD matrix}
    \begin{equation*}
        \A := \left[\xi_k\right]_{k=1}^N \in \R^{N_h \times N}.
    \end{equation*}
    This matrix is $\G$-orthonormal by construction.
    Further, if $N_s, N_t = \infty$, we define the \emph{continuous correlation matrix} via
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: continuous correlation matrix}
        K_\infty = \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} u(\mu, t) \G u^T(\mu, t) d(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h \times N_h},
    \end{equation}
    and its real eigenvalues by $\sigma_{k, \infty}^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{Generalized Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)} \label{algo: pod}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require snapshot matrix $U = [U_j]_{j=1}^{N_s} \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}}$, 
        mass matrix $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$,
        reduction $N < N_h$
        \Ensure POD basis matrix $\A^\mu = [\xi^\mu_1, \dots, \xi^\mu_N] \in \R^{N_h \times N}$
        \If{$N_\text{data} \leq N_h$}
            \State Form the correlation matrix $C = \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} U^T \G U
            \in \R^{N_\text{data} \times N_\text{data}}$
            \State Solve the eigenvalue problem $C \psi_i = \sigma_i^2 \psi_i$, \quad $i=1,\dots,r$
            \State Set $\xi_i = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_i} U \psi_i$, \quad $i=1,\dots,r$
        \Else
            \State Form the correlation matrix $K = \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} 
            U \G U^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$
            \State Solve the eigenvalue problem $K \xi_i = \sigma_i^2 \xi_i$, \quad $i=1,\dots,r$
        \EndIf
        \State $\A \gets \text{stack}(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N)$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


In addition, we can further explore the relation between the discrete correlation matrix and
its singular values attained as a by-product from Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod}) and the continuous 
correlation matrix defined above. A fact used in the proof, but not named in the following proposition
is the qualitative assessment, that the limit singular values are, in general, smaller for each tail sum,
which is interesting to note in the context of Kolmogorov $N$-width with the result following it.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: existence and convergence to sigma infty}
    Let $K$ and $K_\infty$ and their respective eigenvalues be defined as in Definition (\ref{def: POD}), 
    then letting $N_s, N_t \to \infty$, we get 
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{k > N} \sigma_k^2 \longrightarrow \sum_{k > N} \sigma_{k, \infty}^2, \quad \mbox{ a.s.}
    \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $N_s, N_t < \infty$, conforming to the sampling assumption, and 
    \begin{equation*}
        M := 
        \underset{(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times \mathcal{T}}{\operatorname{ess}\, \sup}
        \norm{u(\mu, t)} < \infty
    \end{equation*} 
    as before (see introduction in this Section 
    (\ref{subsec: reduced basis framework})).
    Taking a variational approach on this finite dimensional space, we know, that 
    there exists a $\G$-orthognormal matrix $V_\infty \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ such that 
    \begin{align*}
        \sum_{k > N} \sigma^2_{k, \infty} &= \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} \norm{u(\mu, t) - V_\infty V^T_\infty \G u(\mu, t)}^2 d(\mu, t) \\
        &= \underset{W \in \R^{N_h \times N}: W^T \G W = I}{\min} \, \int_{\mu \times \mathcal{T}} \norm{u(\mu, t) - W W^T \G u(\mu, t) }^2 d(\mu, t).
    \end{align*}
    Thus, since this is the optimal choice of an approximation matrix for the continuous case and 
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ given by Definition (\ref{def: POD}) is only optimal in the discrete setting and 
    not necessarily the continuous case, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{k > N} \sigma^2_{k, \infty} \leq \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} 
        \norm{u(\mu, t) - \A \A^T \G u(\mu, t)}^2 d(\mu, t) = \sum_{k > N} \sigma^2_k.
    \end{equation*} 
    We then write entry-wise for $m, l = 1, \dots N_h$
    \begin{align*}
        [K_\infty - K]_{ml} :=& \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} [\G^{1/2}u(\mu, t)]_m [\G^{1/2}u(\mu, t)]_l d(\mu, t) \\
        &- \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_s} 
        \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} [\G^{1/2}u(\mu_j, t_k)]_m [\G^{1/2}u(\mu_j, t_k)]_l.
    \end{align*}
    By the uniform iid sampling assumed and the general bound on $\norm{u(\mu, t)} \leq M$ before, 
    we know that with the entry-wise boundedness of the PDE solutions, 
    the resulting matrix is subject to the strong law of large numbers, 
    and thus converges to zero, given the entry-wise matrix norm $\opnorm{\cdot}$ as 
    $N_t, N_s \to \infty$ almost surely.
    
    Now using Bauer-Fike's Theorem (\ref{theo: bauer fike}) with the $\opnorm{\cdot}$-norm, we have upon ordering, 
    that for each $\sigma^2_{k, \infty}$ there is a $\sigma^2_{k}$ in the spectrum of $K$, such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \vert \sigma_k^2  - \sigma_{k, \infty}^2 \vert \leq \kappa(\A_\infty) \opnorm{K - K_\infty},
    \end{equation*}
    where $\kappa(\A_\infty)$ denotes the condition number of the stacked eigenvector matrix of 
    $K_\infty$, i.e. $A_\infty = (\xi_{1, \infty}, \dots, \xi_{N_h, \infty})$. This number is independent of $N_s$ and $N_t$.
    Now by convergence of $\opnorm{K - K_\infty} \to 0$ almost surely for $N_s, N_t \to \infty$, we conclude the proof. 
\end{proof}

Following this, is a known legacy result, which may be the most used statement in the later part of this thesis.
It relates the projection error of the Proper Orthogonal Decomposition with its eigen values. 
These values are attained quite easily, which is important to keep in mind. 


\begin{proposition} \label{prop: eigenvalues and optimal projection under sampling}
    Let $\mathcal{V}_N = \{ W \in \R^{N_h \times N} \, \vert \, W^T \G W = I_N \}$ be the set of all 
    $N$-dimensional $\G$-orthonormal matrices, 
    $N_\text{data} := N_t N_s$ and $\A = [\xi]_{i=1}^N$ be the POD matrix according to Definition
    (\ref{def: POD}), then
    \begin{multline*}
        \frac{\euknorm{}\Theta \times [0, T]}{N_\text{data}}
        \sum\limits_{i \in I, k = 1, \dots, N_t} \norm{u(\mu_j, t_k) - \A \A^T \G u(\mu_j, t_k)}^2 \\
        = \min\limits_{W \in \mathcal{V}_N} \frac{\euknorm{}\Theta \times [0, T]}{N_\text{data}} 
        \sum\limits_{i \in I, k = 1, \dots, N_t} 
        \norm{u(\mu_j, t_k) - W W^T \G u(\mu_j, t_k)}^2 
        = \sum_{j=N+1}^r \sigma_k^2.
    \end{multline*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[p.125--126]{quarteroni2015rb}.
\end{proof}

As already hinted, this result can be understood as an optimality statement under a sample $i \in I, k = 1, \dots, N_t$.
Consequently, this, in combination with Proposition (\ref{prop: existence and convergence to sigma infty}), 
enables us with a possibility to classify parametric partial differential equations regarding their "projectability
onto a linear space" and the minimal dimension, where a significant error can be held. 

\begin{definition}[Linear Kolmogorov $N$-width] \label{def: linear kolmogorov}
    Let $N \in \N$, $N \leq N_h$ and $\mathcal{S}_{N_h} = \{ u(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h} \, 
    \vert \, (\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T] \}$ 
    be some parametric manifold. The linear Kolmogorov $N$-width of $\mathcal{S}_{N_h}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation*}
        d_N(S_{N_h}) = \inf\limits_{V \in \R^{N_h \times N}} \sup\limits_{u \in S_{N_h}} 
        \norm{u - V V^T \G u}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

The Kolmogorov $N$-width can then be generally approximated quite well using a large sample via the tail sums of
the eigen values of the discrete correlation matrix. This quantity then allows us to already be able to assert a priori,
how well any POD-based reduction method will work on the specific PPDE; if the tail sum for a choice of reduction 
dimension $N \leq N_h$ is small, then the POD retains most of the energy and a linear reduction can yield high efficiency.

However, problem with effective non-linear terms, and specifically hyperbolic and transport dominated PPDEs have
the tendency to have slowly decaying Kolmogorov $N$-width, meaning they retain energy only for large choices of $N$.
The reduction must then potentially commit a non-linear mapping, motivating the following definition:

\begin{definition}[Nonlinear Kolmogorov $N$-width] \label{def: nonlinear kolmogorov}
    Let $N \in \N$, $N \leq N_h$ and $\mathcal{S}_{N_h}= \{ u(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h} \, 
    \vert \, (\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T] \}$ 
    be some parametric manifold. The nonlinear Kolmogorov $N$-width of $\mathcal{S}_{N_h}$ is defined as 
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta_N(\mathcal{S}_{N_h}) = \inf\limits_{\psi \in C(\R^{N_h}, \R^N), 
        \psi' \in C(\R^N, \R^{N_h})} \sup\limits_{u \in S_{N_h}} \euknorm{u - \psi(\psi'(u))}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}





\section{Neural Networks} \label{section: background for nn}

In this section, we will introduce the general notion of neural networks. Specifically important for us will be 
concatenations of dense linear layers, which are a specific subclass of networks, the so called 
\emph{Deep Feedforward Neural Network} (DFNN), and the \emph{Convolutional Autoencoder}.

\begin{definition}[Neural Network] \label{def: neural network}
    Let $d, L \in \N$, then a \emph{neural network $\Phi$ with input dimension $d$ and $L$ layers} is defined via 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2), \dots, (A_L, b_L)),
    \end{equation*} 
    such that $N_0 = d$ and $N_1, \dots, N_L \in \N$, where each $A_l$ is an $N_l \times \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} N_k$ dimensional
    matrix, and $b_l \in \R^{N_l}$.
    
    We further define $R_\rho(\Phi): \R^d \to \R^{N_L}$ as a \emph{realizations of $\Phi$ with activation function $\rho$} for some
    arbitrary map $\rho : \R \to \R$ as the result of the following scheme for some input $x \in R^d$:
    \begin{align*}
        x_0 &:= x, \\
        x_l &:= \rho \left( A_l \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c]x_0^T & \cdots & x_{l-1}^T\end{bmatrix}^T + b_l \right), \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1, \\
        x_L &:= A_L \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c]x_0^T & \cdots & x_{l-1}^T\end{bmatrix}^T + b_L,
    \end{align*}
    where $\rho$ acts componentwise, i.e. $\rho(y) := [\rho(y_1), \dots, \rho(y_m)]^T \in \R^m$ for each 
    $y = [y_1, \dots, y_m] \in \R^m$. Note that we can write
    \begin{equation*}
        A_l = \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c]A_{l, x_0} & \cdots & A_{l, x_{l-1}}\end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation*}
    with $A_{l, x_k}$ being a $N_l \times N_k$ dimensional matrix for $k = 0, \dots, l-1$ and $l = 1, \dots, L$.
    This comes in handy to portray the scheme via
    \begin{align*}
        x_l &:= \rho \left( A_{l, x_0} x_0 + \dots + A_{l, x_{l-1}} x_{l-1} + b_l \right), \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1, \\
        x_L &:= A_{L, x_{L-1}} x_{L-1} + b_L.
    \end{align*}
    Moreover call $N_{\Phi} := d + \sum_{l=1}^L N_l$ the \emph{number of neurons} of the network, $L_{\Phi} := L$
    the \emph{number of layers}, and finally $\omega_\Phi := \sum_{l=1}^L (\lzeronorm{A_l} + \lzeronorm{b_l})$ the \emph{number 
    of active weights}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    As a note, we can rewrite known neural network architectures, such as a convolutional autoencoder, 
    as a neural network according to Definition (\ref{def: neural network}), see also \cite[p. 22]{gühring2020nn}.
\end{remark}

From this we can infer a simpler definition, describing a network which omits any connection of non-neighboring
layers.

\begin{definition}[Standard Neural Network] \label{def: standard neural network}
    If a neural network $\Phi = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_L, b_L))$ now has the characteristic
    \begin{equation*}
        A_{l, x_i} = 0, \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L, \mbox{ and } i = 0, \dots, l-2,
    \end{equation*}
    then we call $\Phi$ a \emph{standard neural network}.
\end{definition}

When considering the similarities and differences between two distinct neural networks $\Phi_1 = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_L, b_L))$
and $\Phi_2 = ((\hat{A}_1, \hat{b}_1), \dots, (\hat{A}_L, \hat{b}_L))$, one can
easily see, that if $[A_1]_{ij}= 0$, while $[\hat{A}_1]_{ij} \neq 0$ for some $i, j$, then these networks are
severely different, whereas if they only differ in the specific non-zero weight, they could be recovered from each
other through training. This inspires the definition of \emph{an architecture of a network}.

\begin{definition}[Neural Network Architecture] \label{def: neural network architecture}
    For some $d, L \in \N$, we define a \emph{neural network architecture $\mathcal{A}$ with input dimension $d$
    and $L$ layers} to be a binary neural network, i.e. for all entries of $A_i, b_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i = 1, \dots, L$ of $\mathcal{A}$.

    We further say that a \emph{neural network} $\Phi = ((\hat{A}_1, \hat{b}_1), \dots, (\hat{A}_L, \hat{b}_L))$ has \emph{architecture} $\mathcal{A}$, if
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $N_l(\Phi) = N_l$ for all $l = 1, \dots, L$, and
        \item $[\hat{A}_l]_{ij} \neq 0$ implies $[A_l]_{ij} \neq 0$ for all $l = 1, \dots, L$ with $i = 1, \dots, N_l$ and
        $j = 1, \dots, \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} N_k$.
    \end{enumerate} 
\end{definition}

Since the associated realization of a neural network $R_\rho(\Phi)$ requires the definition of $\rho$, the activation function, we will
define the most commonly used one in literature. It will help a lot with some of the constructions, since it has a simple nature, 
but also provides the network with the necessary non-linearity.

\begin{definition}[ReLU] \label{def: relu activation function}
    The function
    \begin{equation*}
        \rho: \R \to \R; \quad x \mapsto \max(0, x),
    \end{equation*}
    is called \emph{ReLU} (Rectified Linear Unit) \emph{activation function.}
\end{definition}

Given such an activation function, Kurt Hornik provided in 1990 the first contribution to a field, which
would emerge centuries later: Approximation theory. He proved for any $L^p(\mu)$ function, for $\mu$ some finite measure, the existence 
of an arbitrarily close ReLU valued neural network \cite{hornik1991nn}.

\begin{theorem}[Hornik's Theorem] \label{theo: hornik theorem}
    Define for some continuous, unbounded and non-constant actiation function $\rho$ the set of all feedforward neural networks, 
    as
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathcal{N}_d(\rho) := \bigcup_{L=1}^\infty \bigg\{ R_\rho(\Phi): \R^d \to \R \, \big\vert \, \Phi \mbox{ is neural network with } L\mbox{ layers} \bigg\}.
    \end{equation*}
    Then $\mathcal{N}_d(\rho)$ lies dense in $L^p(\mu)$ for every finite measure $\mu$, $p \in (0, \infty)$.
\end{theorem}

The proof of this statement leverages on Hahn Banach, but thus does not provide the specific architecture of
the required neural network, which would be used for such an approximation. We aim significantly higher, namely at 
a bound on the necessary non-zero weights and layers for such an approximation, and will thus
not mention the proof beyond this outline. 

\subsection{Network concatenations}

In this subsection we will discuss the concatenations of multiple neural networks and the effects on the characteristic
numbers of it. The complexity of a concatenated network can be given nicely, when the used activation function is the
ReLU.

\begin{definition}[Network Concatenation] \label{def: network concatenation}
    Let $L_1, L_2 \in \N$ and 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi^1 = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_{L_1}, b_{L_1})), \quad \Phi^2 = ((B_1, c_1), \dots, (B_{L_2}, c_{L_2})),
    \end{equation*}
    be two neural networks such that the input layer of $\Phi^1$ has the same dimension as the output layer of $\Phi^2$.
    Then, $\Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^2$ denotes the following $L_1 + L_2 - 1$ layered network:
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^2 := \left( ((B_1, c_1), \dots, (B_{L_2 - 1}, c_{L_2 - 1})), ((\tilde{A}_1, \tilde{b}_1), \dots, (\tilde{A}_{L_1}, \tilde{b}_{L_1})) \right),
    \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*}
        \tilde{A}_l^1 := \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c|c]A_{l, x_0} \, B_{L_2} & A_{l, x_1} & \cdots & A_{l, x_{l-1}}\end{bmatrix},
        \quad \mbox{and} \quad \tilde{b}_l^1 := A_{l, x_0} \, c_{L_2} + b_l,
    \end{equation*}
    for $l = 1, \dots, L_1$. We call $\Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^2$ the \emph{concatenation} of $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Neutral Neural Network]
    Let $\Phi^{\text{Id}} = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2))$ be a $d$-dimensional neural network, with
    \begin{equation*}
        A_1 := \begin{bmatrix} \text{Id}_{\R^d} \\ -\text{Id}_{\R^d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_1 := 0, \quad
        A_2 := \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c] 0_{\R^{d \times d}} & \text{Id}_{\R^d} & - \text{Id}_{\R^d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_2 := 0.
    \end{equation*}
    Then $R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{Id}}) = \text{Id}_{\R^d}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: network concatenation}
    For any two neural networks $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^1$ according to Definition (\ref{def: network concatenation}), 
    $\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2 := \Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^{\text{id}} \bullet \Phi^2$ is a neural network with $d$-dimensional input, $L_1 + L_2$ layers
    with $\omega_{\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2} \leq 2\omega_{\Phi^1}
    + 2\omega_{\Phi^2}$ active weights, $N_{\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2} \leq 2N_{\Phi^1} + 2N_{\Phi^2}$ neurons. Additionally
    for all $x \in \R^d$, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2)(x) = R_\rho(\Phi^1) \circ R_\rho(\Phi^2)(x).
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This can be proven by direct computiation, see \cite{gühring2019nn}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Network parallelization}

We have shown, that there is a possiblity to simply combine networks such that they show the same behavior as
functions do. To 
further build upon that idea, we will now introduce stacking in the context of defining a network $P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)$
from two networks $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$ such that it
emulates the parallel execution of 
two functions, i.e. $R_\rho(P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2))(x) = (R_\rho(\Phi^1)(x), R_\rho(\Phi^2)(x))$ for all $x \in \R^d$.

\begin{definition}[Network Parallelization] \label{def: network parallelization}
    Let $L_1, L_2 \in \N$ s.t. $L_1 \leq L_2$ and let 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi^1 = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_{L_1}, b_{L_1})), \quad \Phi^2 = ((B_1, c_1), \dots, (B_{L_2}, c_{L_2})),
    \end{equation*}
    be two neural networks with $d$-dimensional input. We call
    \begin{equation*}
        P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2) := \left( (C_1, d_1), \dots, (C_{L_2}, d_{L_2}) \right),
    \end{equation*}
    the \emph{parallelization of $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$} with
    \begin{equation*}
        C_l := 
        \begin{bmatrix}[c|cc|c|cc]
        A_{l,x_0} & A_{l,x_1} & 0 & \cdots & A_{l,x_{l-1}} & 0 \\
        B_{l,x_0} & 0 & B_{l,x_1} & \cdots & 0 & B_{l,x_{l-1}}
        \end{bmatrix},
        \qquad
        d_l :=
        \begin{bmatrix}
        b_l \\
        c_l
        \end{bmatrix},
        \qquad \text{for } 1 \le l < L_1,
    \end{equation*}

    \begin{equation*}
        C_{l} :=
        \begin{bmatrix}[c|cc|c|cc|c|c|c]
        B_{l,x_0} & 0 & B_{l, x_1} & \cdots & 0 & B_{l, x_{L_1 - 1}} & B_{l, x_{L_1}} & \cdots & B_{l, x_{l-1}} \\
        \end{bmatrix},
        \qquad
        d_{l} := c_{l},
    \end{equation*}
    for $L_1 \leq l < L_2$, and
    \begin{equation*}
        C_{L_2} :=
        \begin{bmatrix}[c|cc|c|cc|c|c|c]
        A_{L_1,x_0} & A_{L_1,x_1} & 0 & \cdots & A_{L_1, x_{L_1 - 1}} & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\
        B_{L_2,x_0} & 0 & B_{L_2,x_1} & \cdots & 0 & B_{L_2,x_{L_1-1}} & B_{L_2, x_{L_1}} & \cdots & B_{L_2, x_{L_2 - 1}}
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*}
        d_{L_2} :=
        \begin{bmatrix}
        b_{L_1} \\
        c_{L_2}
        \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation*}

    A similar construction is used for the case $L_1 > L_2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: network parallelization}
    In the setting of Definition (\ref{def: network parallelization}), we have that 
    $P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)$ is a neural network with $d$-dimensional input, $\max\{L_1, L_2\}$ layers, 
    $\omega_{P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)} = \omega_{\Phi^1} + \omega_{\Phi^2}$ active weights and $N_{P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)} = N_{\Phi^1} + N_{\Phi^2} - d$ neurons.
    Additionally, for all $x \in \R^d$, we have that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_{\rho}(P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2))(x) = (R_{\rho}(\Phi^1)(x), R_{\rho}(\Phi^2)(x)).
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    If $L_1 = L_2 = 1$, then the parallelization follows directly. After this the proof can be done inductively,
    starting with an evolution in $L_2$ and expanding to arbitrary $L_1$, 
    which can be done using the separation approach. We omit the proof, here
    because of the cumbersome notational nature. Remark, that the non-zero weights can simply be counted to
    get the postulated equality. See \cite{gühring2019nn}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Standard Neural Networks}

Here, we only want to briefly state an interesting fact, following from the definition of the standard networks, that
motivates the treatment of any neural network architechture as a standard one, omiting the cumbersome nature of complete connectivity.

\begin{corollary} \label{cor: eqivalence nn and standard nn}
    Let $\rho$ be the ReLU activation function. Then there are constants $C_1, C_2 > 0$
    such that for any neural network $\Phi$ with $d$-dimensional input and $L = L(\Phi)$ layers, $N = N(\Phi)$
    neurons and $M = M(\Phi)$ nonzero weights, there is a standard neural network $\Phi_{\text{st}}$ with
    $d$-dimensional input, $L$ layers, at most $C_1 L N$ neurons and $C_2 \cdot (L N + M)$ nonzero weights such that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi)(x) = R_\rho(\Phi_{\text{st}})(x)
    \end{equation*}
    for all $x \in \R^d$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[p.9]{gühring2019nn}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Approximation Theory}

For all further theoretical results we will assume that $\rho$, the activation function, is the ReLU as given
in Definition (\ref{def: relu activation function}) and furthermore define the set of functions bounded in Sobolev norm as
\begin{equation*}
    \Fcal_{n,d,p,B} := \big\{ f \in W^{n,p}((0,1)^d) \, \big\vert \, \snorm{f}[n][p][d] \leq B \big\}.
\end{equation*}

To create a decent outline for this subsection, we will shortly discuss the goal and the steps to be taken;
in order to show a bound on the weights and layers needed for an approximating neural network, we need to
take into account the interpolation error, we may overcome with sufficient regularity. Say we would have a 
function $f$ in the Sobolev space $W^{2, 2}((0,1)^d)$, then it is apparent, that at least $2$ derivatives of $f$ must exists,
in a piecewise, embedded sense, which can be leveraged to get a Taylor expansion around selected points.

This procedure will deduce a quantifiable error for the network approximating the initial
arbitrary Sobolev function $f$ in Sobolev norm, as long as we can bound the error, which the
polynomial approximation produces with regard to the network itself.

This leaves us with two main objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bound the Sobolev function at critical equidistant points, using its Taylor expansion.
    \item Relate a fixed error rate of the approximation polynomial and some neural network, to the networks complexity.
\end{enumerate}

These two objectives are pursued by both Yarotsky \cite{yarotsky2017nn} and Gühring et al \cite{gühring2019nn}, 
even though it is worth noting, that Yarotsky
only provides the special case $s=0, B=1$, since the proof only shows an estimation with a Taylor expansion
regarding the $L^\infty$ norm, missing the crucial step of the interpolation bound to change the norm to the
Sobolev expanded counterpart and additionally
uses the $L^\infty$ norm to approximate multiplication by a neural network. 


Tho we will use the statement of
Gühring later on, which is the extension of Yarotsky to any choice of $s, p$ and $B$, 
we will only give the idea of 
the proof, since it follows the same pattern as Yarotsky's proof, but does include a lot of further 
theoretical setting, which will not be of much use in the actual results presented in this paper.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: approximation by square}
    There are constants $c_1, c_2, c_3 > 0$, such that for all $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$ there is a
    neural network $\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon$ with at most $c_1 \cdot \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ nonzero weights, at most
    $c_2 \cdot \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ layers, at most $c_3 \cdot \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ neurons, and with a $1$-dimensional
    input and output such that for $f(x) := x^2$
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon) - f}[0][\infty][1] \leq \varepsilon,
    \end{equation*}
    and $R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon(0)) = 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Define the "tooth" function $g: [0,1] \to [0,1]$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        g(x) = \begin{cases}
            2x, \quad &\text{for } x < 1/2, \\
            2(1-x), \quad &\text{for } x \geq 1/2,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    and its composition 
    \begin{equation*}
        g_s(x) := \underbrace{g \circ g \circ \dots \circ g(x)}_{s \text{-times}}. 
    \end{equation*}
    Furthermore, from the definition of the ReLU function (refer to (\ref{def: relu activation function})),
    we can also set $g(x) = 2 \rho(x) - 4 \rho(x - 1/2) + 2 \rho(x - 1)$, which is intern, the same as defining
    a neural network with $1$-dimensional input and output via $\Phi = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2))$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        A_1 = \begin{bmatrix}
            \text{Id}_\R \\ \text{Id}_\R \\ \text{Id}_\R
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_1 = \begin{pmatrix}
            0 \\ -1/2 \\ -1
        \end{pmatrix}, \quad
        A_2 = \begin{bmatrix}
            2 & -4 & 2 
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_2 = 0.
    \end{equation*}
    Then it is evident, using Lemma (\ref{lemma: network concatenation}), that $g_s(x) = R_\rho(\underbrace{\Phi \odot \dots \odot \Phi}_{s \text{-times}})(x)$ 
    for all $x \in \R$ represents a network with $\Ocal(s)$ layers, active weights and neurons. Now, with an
    explicit formulation of $g_m$ for a fixed level $m \geq 1$, one can identify the function $f(x):=x^2$
    using linear interpolation on equidistant points 
    $k / (2^m)$, where $k = 0, \dots, 2^m - 1$, i.e. let $\Phi_m = \Phi \odot \dots \odot \Phi$ ($m$-times) be a neural network, because then 
    for all $x \in [\frac{k}{2^m}, \frac{k+1}{2^m}]$ it is true, that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi_m)(x) = \left( \frac{(k+1)^2}{2^m} - \frac{k^2}{2^m} \right) \left( x - \frac{k}{2^m} \right)
        + \left( \frac{k}{2^m} \right)^2.
    \end{equation*}
    \almostdone{Why?}
    Then we also know that, using the Bramble-Hilbert lemma in one-dimension \cite{bramble1970}, 
    the fact that $f''(x) = 2$ for all $x \in (0,1)$, and $h = 1/2^m$, that
    \begin{align*}
        \norm{R_\rho(\Phi_m) - f}_{L^\infty((0,1))} \leq \max\limits_{k = 0, \dots, 2^m - 1} \, 
        C h^2 \norm{f''}_{L^\infty([\frac{k}{2^m}, \frac{k+1}{2^m}])} = C' 2^{-2m}.
    \end{align*}
    Hence setting $m = \ceil{\ln(1/\varepsilon)}$, with $C, C' > 0$ constants, only dependent on
    the domain, we achieve the wanted bound from the statement for the neural network
    $\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon := \Phi_m$ with $\Ocal(m) = 
    \Ocal(\ln(1 / \varepsilon) + 1) \leq \Ocal(2 \ln(1 / \varepsilon))$
    layers, neurons and active weights.
\end{proof}

This statement enables us to express multiplication using a neural network. This is straight forward, when 
viewing the polarization identity:
\begin{equation} \label{eq: polarization identify}
    xy = \frac{1}{2}((x + y)^2 - x^2 - y^2), \quad \text{for } x,y \in \R, 
\end{equation}
as given by the first binomial formula.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: quasi multiplication}
    Let $M > 0$ and $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$, then there is a ReLU neural network $\tilde{\times}$ with $2$-dimensional input and
    $1$-dimensional output, and constants $c_1 > 0$ and $c_2 = c_2(M) > 0$, such that for $\times: \R \times \R \to \R; (x, y) \mapsto xy$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\norm{R_\rho(\tilde{\times})- \times}_{W^{0, \infty}((-M, M)^2)} \leq \varepsilon$,
        \item if $x = 0$ or $y = 0$, then $R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y) = 0$,
        \item the number of layers, active weights and neurons of $\tilde{\times}$ is at most $c_1 \ln(1/\varepsilon) + c_2$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\delta := \varepsilon/(6M^2)$. Then we construct
    an approximation of the square function $f(x) = x^2$ for $x \in (0, 1)$ 
    using Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}) for the chosen $\delta$, i.e. we define $\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\delta$,
    such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\delta) - f}[0][\infty][1] \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{6M^2},
    \end{equation*}
    which now of course has $\Ocal(\ln(1/\delta))$ many layers, active weights and neurons. Since the statement
    necessarily needs to implement negative numbers as well, we show with $\vert x \vert = \rho(x) + \rho(-x)$
    that it is indeed possible to expand any neural network with a positive domain to a negative domain using a neural network (by simply concatenating).
    Thus we construct $\tilde{\times}$ via adding an 
    addition layers on top of parallel square networks with scaled input, achieving
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y) = 2 M^2 \left( R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) 
        \left( \frac{\vert x + y \vert}{2M} \right) - 
        R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) \left( \frac{\vert x \vert}{2M} \right)
        - R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) \left( \frac{\vert  y \vert}{2M} \right) \right).
    \end{equation*}
    To appreciate the third statement of our Proposition, 
    we may now put together the necessary complexity of this network using the constants in Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square});
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item We have $2$ layers for the implementation of the absolute value, $1$ for the addition in the end, 
        and $c_0 \ln(1 / \delta)$ for the square function, thus $L_{\tilde{\times}} \leq c_0\ln(1/\varepsilon) + c_0 \ln(6M^2) + 3$.
        \item We need $4 + 2 + 2$ active weights for the first matrix to distribute each $(x,y)$ onto the parallel networks 
        together with $6$ active weights for the superposing of the input into the $\vert \cdot \vert$-function. Another $3$ are required for the
        last layer adding all outputs. Of course each
        of the different computiations for the artificial scaled square function must be in parallel, thus 
        $\omega_{\tilde{\times}} \leq 3c_0\ln(1/\varepsilon) + 3c_0\ln(6M^2) + 17$.
        \item A similar consideration gives us $9 = (2 + 1) \cdot 3$ neurons for the first two and the last layer, thus giving us
        $N_{\tilde{\times}} \leq 3c_0\ln(1/\varepsilon) + 3c_0\ln(6M^2) + 9$.
    \end{enumerate}
    Finally taking the adequate maximum, and remarking, that $c_0$ does not depend on $M$, we get the wanted bound in (2). 


    To make the true multiplication match the form of our implemented network, using (\ref{eq: polarization identify}),
    we may rewrite for $x, y \in (-M, M)$
    \begin{align*}
        xy &= 4M^2 \left(\frac{x}{2M} \cdot \frac{y}{2M} \right) \\
        &= 4M^2 \frac{1}{2} \left( \left( \frac{x}{2M} + \frac{y}{2M} \right)^2 - \left( \frac{x^2}{2M} \right)^2
        \left( \frac{y^2}{2M} \right)^2 \right) \\
        &= 2M^2 \left( \left(\frac{\vert x + y \vert}{2M} \right)^2 - \left( \frac{\vert x \vert}{2M} \right)^2
        - \left( \frac{\vert y \vert}{2M} \right)^2 \right).
    \end{align*}
    Now with the deviation from each of the square approximation we get,
    \begin{align*}
        \norm{R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) - \times}_{L^\infty((0,1)^2)} 
        &\leq 2M^2 \left( 3 \cdot \sup\limits_{\frac{\vert x \vert}{2M}\, : \, x \in (-M, M)}\vert \, R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) 
        (\tilde{x}) - \tilde{x}^2 \, \vert \right) \\
        &\leq 6M^2 \delta = \varepsilon.
    \end{align*}
    We note a few things happening here for clarity; it is possible to simply use the reverse triangle equality
    to get all three term errors added, and then put them together. This can be done, since the $L^\infty((0,1)^2)$ norm 
    appreciates only the maximal value of the error across all possible elements of the $(0,1)^2$ domain, but because
    the inner function is bijective, these are just the same. Additionally we use the, trivial by definition, statement
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{g \circ f}_{L^\infty((0,1))} \leq \norm{g}_{L^\infty((0,1))}.
    \end{equation*}
    This has shown the statement (3).

    Statement (1) 
    on the other hand, is a direct consequence of the subliminally result in Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}).
\end{proof}

As a last step, before stating and proving the Yarotsky Theorem, we want to discuss the construction and complexity
of a network approximating any collection of equidistantly spaced function with local support in a higher dimensional
domain $(0,1)^d$ for $1 \leq d < \infty$. If we take into account, that we may also want these functions
to form a partition of unity on the high dimensional domain, this will be ground to tackle the complexity
of the approximation of polynomials with neural networks.

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: partition of unity}
    For any $d, N \in \N$ there is a collection of functions
    \begin{equation*}
        \Psi = \left\{ \phi_m \, \vert \, m \in \{ 0, \dots, N \}^d \right\},
    \end{equation*}
    with $\phi: \R^d \to \R$ for all $m = (m_1, \dots, m_d) \in \{ 0, \dots, N \}^d$ with the following properties:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $0 \leq \phi_m(x) \leq 1$ for all $\phi_m \in \Psi$ and every $x \in \R^d$,
        \item $\sum_{\phi_m \in \Psi} \phi_m(x) = 1$ for every $x \in [0,1]^d$,
        \item $\text{supp}(\phi_m) \subset B_{1/N, \norm{\cdot}_{\ell^\infty}}(m / N)$ for all $\phi_m \in \Psi$,
        \item there are constants $C, c \geq 1$ such that for each $\phi_m \in \Psi$ there is a neural network
        $\Phi_m$ with $d$-dimensional input and output, at most $3$ layers, $C \cdot d$ active weights and neurons,
        such that
        \begin{equation*}
            \prod_{l=1}^d [R_\rho(\Phi_m)]_l = \phi_m
        \end{equation*}
        and $\norm{[R_\rho(\Phi_m)]_l}_{L^\infty((0,1)^d)} \leq 1$ for all $l = 1, \dots, d$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    The idea of the proof, will be one of construction, where we will find adequate functions, which are
    easily transferable into ReLU neural networks. For this, define the functions
    \begin{equation*}
        \psi: \R \to \R, \quad \psi(x) = \begin{cases}
            1, \quad &\vert x \vert < 1, \\
            0, \quad &2 < \vert x \vert, \\
            2 - \vert x \vert \quad &1 \leq \vert x \vert \leq 2,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    and $\phi_m: \R^d \to \R$ as the prodoct of shifted and scaled versions of $\psi$, which inherit its local
    support, i.e.
    \begin{equation*}
        \phi_m(x) := \prod_{l=1}^d \psi \left( 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right) \right),
    \end{equation*}
    for $m = (m_1, \dots, m_d) \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d$. 
    This function satisfies our first three parts of the Lemma directly: Take one $x_i$ for some $i = 1, \dots, d$.
    Now we get that $\psi(3N(x_i - (m_i/N))) \in [-2, 2]$, since $\psi$ has support of $[-2, 2]$. This gives us
    \begin{equation*}
        x \in \left[\frac{m}{N} - \frac{2}{3N}, \frac{m}{N} + \frac{2}{3N}\right],
    \end{equation*}
    yielding the third property, as we took $i$ arbitrarily. Simultaneously we use the zero product property,
    and the fact, that the range of $\psi$ is $[0, 1]$ to get the first property. Defining $z_i := 3Nx_i$, we
    can rewrite for any $N$ the one-dimensional function 
    \begin{equation*}
        S(x_i) := \sum_{m_i = 0}^N \psi\left( 3N \left( x_i - \frac{m_i}{N} \right) \right)\quad \text{into the function} \quad
        \tilde{S}(z_i) := \sum_{m_i=0}^N \psi(z_i - 3m_i), 
    \end{equation*}
    that intern gives us the wanted result of $\tilde{S}(z_i) = 1$ for any $z_i \in [0, 3N]$, because the
    least distance between points is $3$, which means going forwards or backwards either lands one outside the support
    of the function, if $z_i \in [-1, 1]$, or it adds both third case values, i.e. setting $z_i \in [1, 2]$ w.l.o.g
    \begin{equation*}
        \psi(z_i) + \psi(z_i - 3) = (2 - z_1) + (2 + (z_i - 3)) = 1.
    \end{equation*}
    Now we can extrapolate this result to $S$ by a simple scaling argument and then use Fubini
    and the factorization of the sum over products to get
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \phi_m(x) = \sum_{m_1 = 0}^N \cdots \sum_{m_d = 0}^N \prod_{l=1}^d
        \psi\left( 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right) \right) = \prod_{l=1}^d \left( \sum_{m_l = 0}^N
        \psi\left( 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right) \right) \right) = 1.
    \end{equation*}
    Finally we are only left with proving the forth property. For this, we need to construct a neural network 
    $\Phi_\psi$, that realizes $\psi$. Let
    \begin{equation*}
        A_1 := \begin{bmatrix}
            1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_1 := \begin{pmatrix}
            2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \\ -2
        \end{pmatrix}, \quad
        A_2 := \begin{bmatrix}[c|cccc]
            0 & 1 & -1 & -1 & 1
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_2 := 0,
    \end{equation*}
    define the neural network $\Phi_\psi = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2))$. With distinction by cases one can easily
    see, that for all $x \in \R$
    \begin{align*}
        R_\rho(\Phi_\psi)(x) &= A_2(\rho(A_1x + b_1)) + b_2 \\
        &= A_2\left(\begin{bmatrix}
            \rho(x + 2) & \rho(x + 1) & \rho(x - 1) & \rho(x - 2)
        \end{bmatrix}^T\right) \\
        &= \rho(x + 2) + \rho(x - 2) - (\rho(x + 1) + \rho(x - 1)) \\
        &= \psi(x).
    \end{align*}
    Furthermore the complexity of this network is $2$ layers, $12$ active weights and $6$ neurons.
    Let $\Phi_{m, l}$ be a neural network with $d$-dimensional input and one-dimensional output, such that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi_{m, l})(x) = 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right), 
        \quad \text{for all } x \in \R^d \text{ and } m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d.
    \end{equation*}
    Then the complexity of this network is $1$ layer, $2$ active weights and $d+1$ neurons. Finally realizing
    that the composition of both these functions, i.e. the realization of the concatenation of networks, would yield
    each factor for the wanted function. If we now parallelize this concatenation, we get the forth property
    by definition, i.e. let 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi_m := P(\Phi_\psi \odot \Phi_{m, l} \, \vert \, l = 1, \dots, d),
    \end{equation*}
    since then
    \begin{equation*}
        \prod_{l=1}^d [R_\rho(\Phi_m)]_l(x) = \phi_m(x), \quad \text{for all }x \in \R^d.
    \end{equation*}
    This network now has $d$-dimensional input and output and the complexity, using both Lemma (\ref{lemma: network concatenation}) and Lemma (\ref{lemma: network parallelization}),
    of $3$ layers, at most $(12 + 2) \cdot 2 \cdot d = 28 d$ active weights, and at most $9d$ neurons.
\end{proof}

With these auxiliary results, we may finally start with the statement and proof of the Yarotsky Theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Yarotsky Theorem] \label{theo: yarotsky}
    Let $d \in \N, n \in \N, B = 1$ and $f \in \Fcal_{n ,d , \infty, B}$ some arbitrary function.
    Then there is a constant $c = c(d, n) > 0$ with the following properties:
    
    For any $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$, there is a neural network architecture 
    $\Acal_\varepsilon = \Acal_\varepsilon(d, n, \varepsilon)$ with $d$-dimensional input and one-dimensional output such
    that, there is a neural network $\Phi_\varepsilon^f$ that has architecture $\Acal_\varepsilon$ and satisfies
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{f - R_\rho(\Phi_\varepsilon^f)}[0][\infty][d] \leq \varepsilon,
    \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $L_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \ln(1 / \varepsilon)$,
        \item $\omega_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$,
        \item $N_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Given the definition of the functions $\phi_m \in \Psi$ as constructed in the 
    proof of Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}), we want to structure the proof of this Theorem in
    two sub-goals:
    
    The formulation of an approximate function $f_1$ for $f$ using the partition of unity and
    an adequate Taylor expansion, 
    and the subsequent approximation of that function $f_1$ with a neural network $\Phi_{f_1}$ 
    using the $\tilde{\times}$-multiplication of Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}).

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \noindent\textit{Step 1: Taylor Expansion}
    
    For any $m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d$, consider the $(n-1)$-Taylor polynomial for the function $f$ at
    $x_0 = m/N$:
    \begin{equation*}
        P_m(x) = \sum_{n: \, \vert n \vert < n} \frac{D^n f(x)}{n!} \bigg\vert_{x = \frac{m}{N}} \left( x - \frac{m}{N} \right)^n,
        \quad \text{for } x \in \R^d,
    \end{equation*}
    with the conventions $n! = \prod_{l=1}^d n_l!$ and $(x - m/N)^n = \prod_{l=1}^d(x_l - \frac{m_l}{N})^{n_l}$.
    With the partition of unity of Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}), we can then set any function,
    as a linear combination of its point-values and the partition function. Especially this allows us
    to define
    \begin{equation*}
        f = \sum_{m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \phi_m f, \quad \text{and} \quad f_1 = \sum_{m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \phi_m P_m.
    \end{equation*}
    Now we can exploit the properties given by Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}), as follows;
    firstly use the $\inftynorm{\phi_m} = 1$ property in Line (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 1}), then one can estimate the 
    maximal number of partitions with $2^d$ in Line (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 2}). After this we take the
    simple Lagrange remainder \notdone{argue this} in Line (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 3}).
    Lastly one can simply argue with $f \in \Fcal_{n, d, \infty, B=1}$ in Line (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 4}).
    \begin{align}
        \inftynorm{f - f_1} &\leq \sum_{m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} 
        \inftynorm{\phi_m \left( f - P_m \right)} \\
        &\leq \sum_{m: \left\vert x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right\vert < \frac{1}{N}, \, \forall l = 1, \dots, d} 
        \inftynorm{f - P_m} \label{align: f-f1 eq 1}\\
        &\leq 2^d \max\limits_{m: \left\vert x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right\vert < \frac{1}{N}, \, \forall l = 1, \dots, d}
        \inftynorm{f - P_m} \label{align: f-f1 eq 2}\\
        &\leq \frac{2^d d^n}{n!} \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^n \, 
        \max\limits_{n: \, \vert n \vert = n} \inftynorm{D^n f} 
        \label{align: f-f1 eq 3}\\
        &\leq \frac{2^d d^n}{n!} \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^n \label{align: f-f1 eq 4}
    \end{align}
    Now taking
    \begin{equation*}
        N = \ceil{\left( \frac{n!}{2^d d^n} \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \right)^{-\frac{1}{n}}}
    \end{equation*}
    gives the approximation bound of 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: f-f1 bound in yarotsky}
        \inftynorm{f - f_1} \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
    \end{equation}

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \noindent\textit{Step 2: Approximation by a Neural Network}

    The polynomial function $f_1$ may be further expressed in a different form, which allows us to
    seperately compare it to a potential network approximation using the definition of $\phi_m$, i.e. for
    $x \in \R^d$ 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: yarotsky f_1 def}
        f_1(x) = \sum_{m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \sum_{n: \, \vert n \vert < n} a_{m, n} 
        \underbrace{\left( \phi_m(x) \left(x - \frac{m}{N}\right)^n \right)}_{=: f_{m, n}(x)}.
    \end{equation}
    This expansion now has at most $d^n (N + 1)^d$ terms containing $f_{m, n}$, since 
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\vert\left\{ n \in \{0, \dots, n - 1\}^d \, : \, \vert n \vert < n\right\}\right\vert \\
        \leq \left\vert\left\{ n \in \{0, \dots, n - 1\}^d \right\}\right\vert = d^n,
    \end{equation*}
    and obviously $\vert\{m = \{0, \dots, N\}^d \}\vert = (N + 1)^d$. We note, that we take $M = d + n$ for the
    definition of the network multplication, i.e. $\tilde{\times}: (-M, M)^2 \to \R$. This is done, because
    we have as an obvious consequence from the fact, that if $x, y \in (-1, 1)$, that
    \begin{equation*}
        \vert \, R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y) - x y \, \vert \leq \delta \quad
        \implies \quad
        \vert \, R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y)\, \vert \leq \vert y \vert + \delta,
    \end{equation*}
    for some fixed but not yet specified $\delta \in (0,1)$. Recursively concatenating 
    $\tilde{\times}$ for $(d + n - 1)$-times can then propagate the error to at most $M = (d + n)$ 
    upon the last realization of $\tilde{\times}$.
    
    As aforementioned, we can now compare each
    function $f_{m,n}$ pointwise to a realization of a network approximating at most $n - 1$ of the multiplications
    needed for $\prod_{l=1}^d (x_l - (m_l / N))^{n_l}$, as we know that $\sum_{l=1}^d n_l < n$, 
    $d$ additional multiplications for the realization
    of the function $\phi_m$ and one multiplication for putting it together. Hence we define a neural
    network $\Phi^{m, n}_{f_1}$ with $d$-dimensional input and one dimensional output via
    \begin{multline*}
        R_\rho(\Phi^{m, n}_{f_1})(x) = R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\bigg( [R_\rho(\Phi_m)]_1(x_1), 
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\bigg([R_\rho(\Phi_m)]_2(x_2), \dots \\
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) \bigg([R_\rho(\Phi_m)]_d(x_d), R_\rho(\tilde{\times})
        \bigg(x_1 - \frac{m_1}{N}, \dots \\
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) \bigg( x_{d-1} - \frac{m_{d-1}}{N}, R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\bigg(
        x_d - \frac{m_d}{N}, \dots \\
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) \bigg(x_d - \frac{m_d}{N},  x_d - \frac{m_d}{N}\bigg)
        \bigg)\bigg)\bigg)\bigg) \bigg)\bigg).
    \end{multline*}
    for $x = (x_1, \dots, x_d) \in \R^d$ where we used $\Phi_m$ as in the proof Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}).
    This network contains at most $(d + n)$ concatenations of $\tilde{\times}$, 
    and thus by Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}) and its constant $C > 0$, Lemma (\ref{lemma: network concatenation}),
    and Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}) and its constants $c_0, c_1(d, n) > 0$,
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: yarotsky complexity with delta}
        L_{\Phi^{m, n}_{f_1}} \leq 3 c_0 (d + n) \left(\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right) + (d + n) c_1 \right), \quad
        \omega_{\Phi^{m, n}_{f_1}} \leq C 2(d + n) d \left(\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right) + 2(d + n) C \right).
    \end{equation}
    for a given precision level $\delta \in (0, 1)$, where the number of neurons are of the same order as 
    the number of active weights.

    The error comparison of $R_\rho(\Phi^{m, n}_{f_1})$ and $f_{m, n}$ can then be done by counting the maximal depth of 
    usages of the network $\tilde{\times}$,
    which is $(d + n)$ as shown above, and the estimate of 
    \begin{equation*}
        \bigg\vert \, \left(x - \frac{m}{N}\right) \, \bigg\vert \leq 1, \quad 
        \vert \, \psi(x) \, \vert \leq 1, 
    \end{equation*} 
    for all $x \in (0, 1)^d$. Then directly
    \begin{equation*}
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{m, n}) - f_{m,n}} \leq (d + n) \delta.
    \end{equation*}
    Now since, as mentioned below Equation (\ref{eq: yarotsky f_1 def}), $f_1$ has at most $d^n (N + 1)^d$
    terms, but since any $x \in \R^d$ can be only in the support of at most $2^d$ functions $\phi_m$ by the
    second statement in Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}), the
    sum going over all $m$ can be reduced to only those, which are non-zero, which we employ in 
    Line (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 1}). This brings
    $(N+1)^d \to 2^d$, supporting the equation in Line (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 2}). Setting
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{2 \cdot 2^{d} d^n(d + n)}
    \end{equation*}
    allows us to finally assert, using the bound of Equation (\ref{eq: yarotsky complexity with delta})
    for Line (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 3}),
    \begin{align}
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}) - f_1}
        &\leq \sum_{m \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} 
        \sum_{n: \, \vert n \vert < n} \vert a_{m, n}  \vert \,
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{m, n}) - f_{m, n}} \\
        &= \sum_{m: \, x \in \operatorname{supp}(\phi_m)} 
        \sum_{n: \, \vert n \vert < n} \vert a_{m, n} \vert \,
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{m, n}) - f_{m, n}} \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 1}\\
        &\leq 2^d d^n \max\limits_{m: \, x \in \operatorname{supp}(\phi_m)} \max\limits_{n: \, \vert n \vert < n}
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{m, n}) - f_{m, n}} \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 2}\\
        &\leq 2^d d^n (d + n) \delta \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 3} \\
        &= \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 4}
    \end{align}
    Our knowledge of $\delta$ together with at most $d^n (N + 1)^d = \Ocal(\varepsilon^{-d/n})$ parallel networks 
    with one additive layer and the fact that
    generally we have $c'\ln(1 / \delta + c'') \leq c''' \ln(1 / \delta)$ 
    for $c', c'' > 0$ and $c''' \geq c' \ln(1 / c'')$, gets us the final complexity of $\Phi_{f_1}$ 
    from the starting point of
    Equation (\ref{eq: yarotsky complexity with delta}), i.e.
    \begin{equation*}
        L_{\Phi_{f_1}} \leq c \ln(1 / \varepsilon), \quad \omega_{\Phi_{f_1}} \leq c \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon),
        \quad N_{\Phi_{f_1}} \leq c \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon).
    \end{equation*}
    with some constant $c = c(d, n) > 0$.

    Using the triangle inequality, we can show that this network indeed satisfies the wanted precision bound with
    both errors stemming from Equation () and Equation () respectively:
    \begin{equation*}
        \inftynorm{f - R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1})} \leq \inftynorm{f - f_1} + \inftynorm{f_1 - R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1})}
        \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

We may even expand Yarotsky to cover a more general case, where $f \in \Fcal_{n, d, p, B}$ for arbitrarily
$1 \leq p \leq \infty$ and $B > 0$ and the norm, to which we assert the precision, an arbitray Sobolev norm.
This extension is presented by Gühring et al. \cite{gühring2019nn}.

\begin{theorem}[Gühring Theorem] \label{theo: gühring}
    Let $d \in \N, n \in \N_{\geq 2}, B > 0$ and $0 \leq s \leq 1$ and $f \in \Fcal_{n ,d , p, B}$ some arbitrary function.
    Then there is a constant $c = c(d, n, p, B, s) > 0$ with the following properties:
    
    For any $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$, there is a neural network architecture 
    $\Acal_\varepsilon = \Acal_\varepsilon(d, n, p, B, s, \varepsilon)$ with $d$-dimensional input and one-dimensional output such
    that, there is a neural network $\Phi_\varepsilon^f$ that has architecture $\Acal_\varepsilon$ and satisfies
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{f - R_\rho(\Phi_\varepsilon^f)}[s][p][d] \leq \varepsilon,
    \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $L_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \ln(1 / \varepsilon)$,
        \item $\omega_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/(n-s)} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$,
        \item $N_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/(n-s)} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof Sketch]
    Considering that all of the tools used for this proof will neither be of any use to the main results in
    this paper nor to the general structure, we will only sketch the general steps necessary to achieve
    it for the interested reader:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The first step is to expand the Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}) to the 
        $W^{1, \infty}(0,1)$-norm. This is done by simple calculation, since an explicit construction is being made.
        \item This can be applied to Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}) in the same way. Additionally
        we need to bound $\vert\,R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\, \vert_{W^{1, \infty}}((0,1)^2)$, since this is required in
        for the bound on the collective error under the Taylor expanded function in the end.
        \item The most import additional step is to bound the switch of norms from $W^{1, p}((0,1)^d)$ to 
        $W^{s, p}((0,1)^d)$ of a function being approximated by the partition of unity, that we discussed
        in Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}). This gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \snorm{f - f_N}[s][p][d] \leq C \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^{n-s} \snorm{f}[n][p][d],
        \end{equation*}
        for a partition of unity of size $N$. It is worth noting, that this factor in front is responsible for the
        different approximation in regard to weights and neurons, since it influences the necessary choice of $\delta$.
        \item The last step, which differs greatly from the proof of Yarotsky Theorem (\ref{theo: yarotsky}), is
        the approximation of a sum of localized polynomials with a neural network. Here the challenge lies again in
        the control of the derivatives.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}




\subsection{Statistical Learning} \label{subsec: statistical learning}




We have now introduced the framework for analytical discussion regarding complexity and potential of neural networks.
What is clearly missing however, is the practical "achievement" of a true neural network $\Phi$ fitting the 
function in question from some fixed
architecture $\Acal_\Phi$. This crucial step in the process of 
development is refered to as \emph{module training} or \emph{statistical learning}.

In order to quantify the "falseness" of a potential attribution, we need a function, that compares a true solution
to an output of the network operating with the true parameters connected to the true solution. We remark that
the following two definitions are based upon the paper \cite{ciampiconi2024}, where the interested reader
can find extensive terminology and currently used loss functions and their optimization techniques. 

\begin{definition}[Feature and Label Space] \label{def: feature and label space}
    Let $\mathcal{X}$ be the so-called \emph{feature space} and $\mathcal{Y}$ the \emph{label space} of the underlying
    unknown function
    \begin{equation*}
        f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y},
    \end{equation*}
    classifying each feature with a label. 
\end{definition}
The general goal of all machine learning problems is to closely approximate that classification function $f$.

\begin{definition}[Loss Function]
    Given $f_\theta: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ arbitrarily and a feature and label space as in Definition (\ref{def: feature and label space}) and a sample
    $\{x_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N \subset \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ for some $N \in \N$, we define
    the general loss function as a mapping of $f(x_i)$ with its corresponding $y_i$ to a non-negative real number, i.e.
    \begin{equation*}
        \Lcal(f_\theta \vert \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \{y_1, \dots, y_N\}) \in \R^+,
    \end{equation*}
    such that for $f$ being the function from Definition (\ref{def: feature and label space}) for all $N \in \N$
    \begin{equation*}
        \Lcal(f \vert \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \{y_1, \dots, y_N\}) = 0.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Neural Network Weights] \label{def: neural network weights}
    Suppose for some $d, L \in \N$ we have a neural network architecture $\Acal_\Phi$ 
    of input dimension $d$, $L$ layers and output 
    dimension $N_L$ according to Definition (\ref{def: neural network}) and $\Phi$ be a neural network of
    that architecture (see Definition (\ref{def: neural network architecture})).
    Define the \emph{masks of $\Acal_\Phi$} via
    \begin{equation*}
        I_l := \{ (i, j) \, : \, [A_l]_{ij} \neq 0 \}, \quad J_l := \{i \, : \, [b_l]_i \neq 0 \},
    \end{equation*}
    for each layer $1 \leq l \leq L$. We further set the \emph{admissible weight space} as
    \begin{equation*}
        \Theta(\Acal_\Phi) := \prod_{l=1}^L \left( \R^{I_l} \times \R^{J_l} \right),
    \end{equation*}
    and its elements $\theta \in \Theta(\Acal_\Phi)$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        \theta = \left( (\theta_{l, ij}^A)_{(i, j) \in I_l}, (\theta_{l, i}^b)_{i \in J_l} \right)_{l=1}^L.
    \end{equation*}
    Then in each layer of $\Phi(\theta) = 
    ((\hat A_1(\theta), \hat b_1(\theta)), \dots, (\hat A_L(\theta), \hat b_L(\theta)))$
    the affine transformation can be uniquely retrieved via the lifting
    \begin{equation*}
        \hat A_l(\theta)_{ij} = 
        \begin{cases}
            \theta^A_{l,ij}, & (i,j)\in I_l,\\
            0, & \text{otherwise},
        \end{cases}
        \qquad
        \hat b_l(\theta)_{i} =
        \begin{cases}
            \theta^b_{l,i}, & i\in J_l,\\
            0, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    Finally, this allows us to classify realizations of a neural network uniquely as
    a map, when knowing
    its architecture $\Acal_\Phi$ and a choice of neural network weights 
    $\theta \in \Theta(\Acal_\Phi)$, via
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi(\theta)) : \R^d \to \R^{N_L}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}


We will often come in contact with this definition, since the goal of all approximation theory is to
state theorems in terms of "existence of an architecture, such that there is a choice of neural
network weights, attaining a close approximation of some function $f$". This definition of neural network
weights relates any two neural networks with the same architecture, as simply the same one at
a different stage of training, i.e. with a different choice of weights. With this in mind, the natural
next question is, how one could achieve a better choice of weights after the first inital guess is not
sufficient. This simple question is one, which can not be easily answered. However, we will
provide the reader with a crude overview of the techniques, we will be using in our implementation.
The main goal here, is to minimize the associated loss function for some neural network $\Phi$ with architechture
$\Acal_\Phi$ with regard to some optimal function $f$ we want to approximate, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
    \underset{\theta \in \Theta(\Acal_\Phi)}{\operatorname{arg \, min}} \,\, 
    \Lcal\left(R_\rho(\Phi(\theta)) \, \vert \,  \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \{f(x_1), \dots, f(x_N)\}\right)
\end{equation*}

In the following, a brief introduction to the topics of batching, normalization, optimizers and
schedulars is given.

\paragraph{Batching}

The term \emph{batch} refers to a stack of a sample $\{x_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N \subset \Xcal \times \mathcal{Y}$.
This usually means, stacking along a new dimension. Conventionally this is the first dimension and the
batches are chosen as large as possible, but such that a clustering of features is avoided.

\paragraph{Normalization}

Even though the world of approximation theoretic mathematics 
does not care about the specific order of numbers, the world
of computer science begs to differ, since scaling can affect the problem; in fact, regions of low magnitude
can be ignored completely by the optimizer, when high value regions sway the loss function 
in an overwhelming way, thus making the optimizer only consider those. This can be mitigated, by 
a priori normalizing the input data and the output data. Consider, for example, a one-dimensional sample 
$\{x_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N \subset \R \times \R$,
then we define the \emph{min-max normalization} via 
\begin{equation*}
    x_i \mapsto \frac{x_i - x_\text{min}}{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}} \in [0, 1], \quad y_i \mapsto 
    \frac{y_i - y_\text{min}}{y_\text{max} - y_\text{min}} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation*}
where we setup the maximum and minimum via
\begin{equation*}
    x_\text{max} = \max\limits_{i = 1, \dots, N} x_i, \quad x_\text{min} = \min\limits_{i = 1, \dots, N} x_i,
\end{equation*}
and for $y_\text{min}, y_\text{max}$ accordingly. It is important to remember to 
denormalize the solution for evalution and comparative analysis with the deterministic inverse
operation. For our later example, we will switch this into
a multidimensional setting.

\paragraph{Optimizer}

The choice of optimization method is crucial for the practical performance of neural networks. 
The original gradient descent scheme with stochastic approximation dates 
back to Robbins and Monro \cite{robbins1951}, 
and became widely adopted in the form of backpropagation \cite{rumelhart1986}. 
A first improvement was the introduction of momentum terms \cite{qian1999}, 
followed by adaptive step-size methods such as 
Adagrad \cite{duchi2011} and RMSProp \cite{tieleman2012}. 
The most popular adaptive optimizer in modern deep learning is Adam \cite{kingma2015}, 
which combines momentum with adaptive learning rates, and its decoupled variant AdamW 
for improved regularization \cite{loshchilov2019}. 
In our implementations we will mainly rely on AdamW.

\paragraph{Schedular}

Learning rate schedulers control the step-size of the optimizer during training. 
While constant learning rates are sufficient for convergence in theory, 
in practice carefully designed schedules can significantly improve both training stability 
and generalization. 
One well-established approach is the use of \emph{cosine annealing with warm restarts} (SGDR) 
introduced by Loshchilov and Hutter \cite{loshchilov2017}, 
which periodically reduces and resets the learning rate. 
Another popular technique is the class of cyclical learning rates \cite{smith2017}. 
In this work, we adopt cosine warm restarts due to their good empirical performance on PDE-related problems.
