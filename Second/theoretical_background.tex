\section{Linear Algebra} \label{section: linear algebra}


For this section, we will specifically set up the euclidean (Hilbert) space $(\R^{N_h}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G)$,
with the inner product
\begin{equation*}
    \langle u, v \rangle_\G := u^T \G v,
\end{equation*}
for some symmetric positive definite $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ for each $u, v \in \R^{N_h}$ and
$\norm{\cdot}$ its induced norm. 
As preparation for the upcoming Bauer Fike Theorem, we further want to define the matrix norm for the 
matrix space $\R^{N_h \times N}$,
where $N \leq N_h$, as follows
\begin{equation*}
    \opnorm{A} := \sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(A^T \G A)}.
\end{equation*}
Here, $\lambda_\text{max}(A^T \G A)$ is the maximal spectral radius of $A^T \G A$, 
which exists because $A^T \G A$ is symmetric and positive semi definite
and hence subject to the spectral theorem.
This norm satisfies the wanted property mentioned in the original paper \cite[p. 137-141]{bauer1960la}. Namely, 
for all $x \in \R^{N}$, we have
\begin{equation*}
    \norm{Ax}^2 = x^T A^T \G A x \leq \lambda_\text{max}(A^T \G A) x^T x \leq \opnorm{A}^2 \euknorm{x}^2.
\end{equation*} 

\begin{theorem}[Bauer-Fike Theorem] \label{theo: bauer fike}
    Let $K \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be some diagonalizable matrices with $\sigma_k$ eigenvalues and 
    $V = (\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N) \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be the stacked eigenvector matrix of $K$. Let 
    $\delta K \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be some symmetric matrix. Then if $\lambda_{k}$ are the eigenvalues of 
    $K + \delta K$, we have 
    \begin{equation*}
        \min\limits_{k} \vert \lambda_k - \sigma_k \vert \leq \kappa(V) \opnorm{(K + \delta K) - K},
    \end{equation*}
    where $\kappa(V) = \opnorm{V} \opnorm{V^{-1}}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[p. 137-141]{bauer1960la}.
\end{proof}

Furthermore, we will often discuss the notion of orthonormality with regard to a a matrix. This
can be understood with the following definition:

\begin{definition}[Matrix Orthonormality] \label{def: matrix orthonormality}
    Let $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be a symmetric positive definite matrix for some $N_h \in \N$. We then say that
    any matrix $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ is $\G$-orthonormal for some $N \leq N_h$, if
    \begin{equation*}
        \I = \A^T \G \A.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    If some matrix $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ can be written as $\V = \A \tilde{\V}$, where
    $\tilde{\V} \in \R^{N_A \times N}$ is any matrix and $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ is a $\G$-orthonormal matrix, 
    then $\V$ is $\G$-orthonormal if and only if $\tilde{\V}$ is orthonormal in the euclidean sense. 
    In fact,
    \begin{equation*}
        \V^T \G \V = \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G \A \tilde{\V} = \tilde{\V}^T \tilde{\V}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Orthogonal Projector] \label{def: orthogonal projector}
    Let $P: V \to V$ be an operator, where $V$ is a Hilbert space with inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_V$.
    Then it 
    is called an \emph{orthogonal projector} onto $W \subset V$, if
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $P^2 = P$,
        \item $\text{Im}(P) = W$ and
        \item $\langle Px, y \rangle_V = \langle x, Py \rangle_V$ for all $x, y \in V$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: orthogonal projector on span of A}
    Let $(\R^{N_h}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G)$ be the Hilbert space from above. If
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ is a $\G$-orthonormal matrix, then
    \begin{equation*}
        P := \A \A^T \G
    \end{equation*}
    is a orthogonal projector on $\text{span}(\A)$ with regard to the $\G$ induced inner product.
    We refer to this as $P$ being the \emph{$\G$-orthonormal projector} on $\text{span}(\A)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    It suffices to simply check the properties of Definition (\ref{def: orthogonal projector}); the image
    statement holds, since $\A \A^T \G = \A (\A^T \G)$. Next, the idempotency can be
    shown via
    \begin{equation*}
        P^2 = (\A \A^T \G)(\A \A^T \G) = \A (\A^T \G \A) \A^T \G = \A \A^T \G = P.
    \end{equation*}
    Finally for $u, v \in \R^{N_h}$ arbitrary, we have 
    \begin{align*}
        \langle P u, v \rangle_\G &= (P u)^T \G v \\
        &= u^T P^T \G v \\
        &= u^T (\A \A^T \G)^T \G v \\
        &= u^T \G \A \A^T \G v \\
        &= u^T \G P v = \langle u, Pv \rangle_\G.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: opnorm A}
    Let $(\R^{N_h}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G)$ be the Hilbert space from above.
    If now
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ is a $\G$-orthonormal matrix, then
    \begin{equation*}
        \opnorm{\A} = 1.
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This is directly clear, since $\opnorm{\A} = \sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(\A^T \G \A)} =
    \sqrt{\lambda_\text{max}(\I)} = 1$.
\end{proof}


These preliminaries will play an important role, since we will use such a 
gramian $\G$ of an a priori chosen basis under a discretization of a PDE to set up a linear projector.
This projector will
attain any coefficient representation onto the solution manifold in consideration of that gramian $\G$.
In fact, any projector 
\begin{equation*}
    \V \V^T \G : \R^{N_h} \to \R^N
\end{equation*}
for a $\G$-orthonormal matrix $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ and some $N \leq N_h$ can be seen as a special
case for the differential geometric framework 
of (nonlinear) projectors on manifolds. This is discussed in more detail in \cite{buchfink2023}.

\subsection{Sobolev Spaces}




For our work with partial differential equations, and subsequent error approximations, we introduce
Sobolev spaces. These are generalized differentiable spaces, where a weak derivative is given, as an analog to the
classic derivative in the $C^n(\Omega)$ 
space. They are employed in order to exploit their compact embedding in the Hölder spaces, which broadly are generalizations
of $C^n(\Omega)$. We will not go in much detail about the theory of Sobolev spaces, since we will focus on
finite dimensional subspaces, but some ground truth must be discussed. 

For this subsection we primary use \cite{alt2016}, and for the first upcoming definition \cite{dacorogna2004}.

\begin{definition}[Lipschitz Domain]
    Let $d \in \N$, and $\Omega \subset \R^d$. Then $\Omega$ is called a \emph{Lipschitz domain} if for every
    $x \in \partial \Omega$ and some $r > 0$, there is $U := B_{r, \vert \cdot \vert}(x) \subset \R^d$ and an isomorphic
    map $H: Q \to B_{r, \vert \cdot \vert}(x)$ with $Q := \{ x \in \R^d \, \vert \, \norm{x}_{\ell^1} < d \}$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
        H \in C^k(\bar{Q}), \quad H^{-1} \in C^k(\bar{U}), \quad 
        H(Q_+) = U \cap \Omega, \quad H(Q_0) = U \cap \partial \Omega.
    \end{equation*}
    Here, $Q_+ := \{x \in Q \, \vert \, x_d > 0\}$ and similarly $Q_0 := \{x \in Q \, \vert \, x_d = 0 \}$,
    and $H$ is only in $C^{0, 1}$.
\end{definition}

From now on, let $d \in \N$ and, if not given otherwise, $\Omega \subset \R^d$ be a Lipschitz domain.

\begin{definition}[Weak Derivative]
    Let $\alpha \in \N_0^d$ be a multiindex. A function $u \in L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)$ is said to have an \emph{$\alpha$-th 
    weak derivative}, if there is a function $v_\alpha \in L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \int_\Omega u D^\alpha \varphi = (-1)^{\vert \alpha \vert} \int_\Omega v_\alpha \varphi \quad \forall
        \varphi \in C_0^\infty(\Omega).
    \end{equation*}
    We then write $v_\alpha = D^\alpha u$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sobolev Space] \label{def: sobolev space}
    Let $n \in \N_0$ and $1 \leq p \leq \infty$. Then we define the \emph{Sobolev space} as
    \begin{equation*}
        W^{n, p}(\Omega) := \{ f \in L^p(\Omega) \, \vert \, \norm{f}_{W^{n, p}(\Omega)} < \infty \},
    \end{equation*}
    where the norm on this space is given via
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{f}_{W^{n, p}(\Omega)} := \begin{cases}
            \displaystyle
            \left( \sum\limits_{\vert \alpha \vert \leq n} \norm{D^\alpha f}^p_{L^p(\Omega)} \right)^{1/p}, 
            \quad &\text{if } p<\infty \\[1.5em]
            \displaystyle
            \, \max\limits_{\vert \alpha \vert \leq n} \, \norm{D^\alpha f}_{L^\infty(\Omega)}, \quad &\text{if } p = \infty,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    for any $f \in W^{n, p}(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

It is possible to extend this definition for $0 < n < 1$, namely the \emph{Sobolev-Slobodeckij spaces}. In the 
analysis of partial differential equations these are used to characterize the regularity of Sobolev functions,
which are restricted to the boundary $\partial \Omega$. This restricted function is then called a trace operator.
One of the statements of relevance for such fractal Sobolev spaces is the following.

Let $\Omega \subset \R^d$ be a regular open set (for example, if $\Omega$ is a Lipschitz domain), and
$u \in W^{1, p}(\Omega)$. Then the trace operator $u \mapsto u \vert_{\partial \Omega}$ is surjective
from $W^{1, p}(\Omega)$ onto $W^{1-(1/p), p}(\partial \Omega)$ and
\begin{equation*}
    \norm{u \vert_{\partial \Omega}}_{W^{1-(1/p), p}(\partial \Omega)} \leq C \norm{u}_{W^{1, p}(\Omega)},
\end{equation*}
for some $C > 0$ independent of $p$ and $u$, see \cite[p. 315]{brezis2011}.
We make sense of this space via the next definition.

\begin{definition}[Sobolev-Slobodeckij Space]
    Let $0 < s < 1$ and $1 \leq p \leq \infty$. Then we define the \emph{Sobolev-Slobodeckij space} as
    \begin{equation*}
        W^{s, p}(\Omega) := \{ f \in L^p(\Omega) \, \vert \, \norm{f}_{W^{s, p}(\Omega)} < \infty \},
    \end{equation*}
    where the norm on this space is given via
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{f}_{W^{s, p}(\Omega)} := \begin{cases}
            \displaystyle
            \left( \norm{f}_{L^p(\Omega)}^p + \int_\Omega \int_\Omega 
            \left( \frac{\vert f(x) - f(y) \vert}{\vert x-y \vert^{s + d/p}}\right)^p dx dy \right)^{1/p}, 
            \quad &\text{if } p<\infty \\[1.5em]
            \displaystyle
            \, \max \left\{\norm{f}_{L^\infty(\Omega)}, \operatorname{ess} \!\! \sup_{x, y \in \Omega} 
            \frac{\vert f(x)-f(y) \vert}{\vert x-y \vert^{s}} \right\}, \quad &\text{if } p = \infty,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    for any $f \in W^{s, p}(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

Furthermore, a very important result is the following theorem, translating Lipschitz continuity into
Sobolev-regularity. It will allow us to employ the later introduced approximation theory for neural networks
from the sole position of Lipschitz continuity.

\begin{theorem} \label{theo: lipschitz implies sobolev}
    Let $f: \Omega \to \R$ be a bounded and $L$-Lipschitz map, then $f \in W^{1, \infty}(\Omega)$, the 
    weak gradient of $f$ agrees almost everywhere with the classical gradient, and
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{D f(x)}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)} \leq L.
    \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[Theorem 4.1]{heinonen2005} and \cite[Remark 4.2]{heinonen2005}.
\end{proof}




\subsection{Reduced Basis Framework}  \label{subsec: reduced basis framework}


For this subsection we define $u(\mu_j, t_k) \in \R^{N_h}$ for $(\mu_j)_{j = 1}^{N_s} =: \Pcal \subset \Theta$ and 
$(t_k)_{k = 1}^{N_t} =: \Tcal \subset [0, T]$ to be some vectors. They will later represent the solutions to a 
parametric PDE for a 
determined parameter set and time point in a given discrete subspace of the coefficient solution manifold. 
Here $\Theta$ represents and arbitrary parameter space, 
$T > 0$ some arbitrary time point, and $N_s, N_t \in \N$ are the parameter and time sample size, respectively. 
In context of the aforementioned coefficient solution manifold,
the inner product on $\R^{N_h}$ is defined by 
using a symmetric positive definite matrix $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ via 
\begin{equation*}
    \langle u, v \rangle_\G := u^T \G v, \qquad \text{for all } u, v \in \R^{N_h},
\end{equation*}
and the corresponding norm $\norm{\cdot} := \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_\G$.

It is assumed,
that we sample the parameters independently and identically distributed, and the time points equidistantly determined
from their respective spaces, and that 
\begin{equation*}
    \sup\limits_{(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T]} \norm{u(\mu, t)} \leq M < \infty, \quad \inf\limits_{(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T]}\norm{u(\mu, t)} \geq m > 0,
\end{equation*}
for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $t \in [0, T]$ in an essential
sense. Let also 
\begin{equation*}
    N_\text{data} := N_s \cdot N_t,
\end{equation*}
be the total sample size.

Notice, that it is possible to switch to a lower dimensional euclidean norm, if the projector in consideration
is an orthogonal projector on the span of a $\G$-orthonormal matrix with specific identity. Namely, this is true,
if the matrix $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ 
for some $N \leq N_h$ is
$\G$-orthonormal and can be expressed as $\V = \A \tilde{\V}$, where $\A \in \R^{N_A \times N}$
for $N \leq N_A \leq N_h$ is also $\G$-orthonormal. This is quantified by the following result.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: switch to euclidean norm}
    Let $\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ 
    for some $N \leq N_h$ be
    $\G$-orthonormal such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \V = \A \tilde{\V},
    \end{equation*}
    where $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$
    is also $\G$-orthonormal and $\tilde{\V} \in \R^{N_A \times N}$ for $N \leq N_A \leq N_h$. 
    Then we have
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{u - \V \V^T \G u}^2 = c_\A + \euknorm{u_\text{pre-red} - \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T u_\text{pre-red}}^2,
    \end{equation*}
    for any 
    $u \in \R^{N_h}$ with $c_\A > 0$ being a constant only depending on $\A$ and defining $u_\text{pre-red} = \A^T \G u$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    By Lemma (\ref{lemma: orthogonal projector on span of A}), we know that $\A \A^T \G$ is a $\G$-orthonormal 
    projector into the span of $\A$. Thus, its residual $u - \A \A^T \G u$ must be 
    $\G$-orthogonal to all columns of $\A$, since we can decompose 
    $u = u_\perp + u_\parallel$, where $u_\parallel \in \text{span}(\A)$ and 
    $u_\perp \in \R^{N_h} - \text{span}(\A)$. Then the Pythagoras decomposition gives
    \begin{align*}
        \norm{u - \V \V^T \G u}^2 &= \norm{u - \A \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G u}^2 \\
        &= \norm{u - \A \A^T \G u}^2 + \norm{\A \A^T \G u - \A \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G u}^2 \\
        &= \norm{u - \A \A^T \G u}^2 + \euknorm{\A^T \G u - \tilde{\V} \tilde{\V}^T \A^T \G u}^2.
    \end{align*}
    The last step is justified by showing that for any $u \in \R^{N_A}$
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{\A u} = \langle \A u, \A u \rangle_\G = (\A u)^T \G \A u = u^T \A^T \G \A u = u^T u = \euknorm{u}.
    \end{equation*}
    Now setting $u_\text{pre-red} := \A^T \G u$ gives us the wanted statement.
\end{proof}

This proposition will be the core idea to
assert closeness with regard to the representations of the solution and
simultaneous provide a computationally
feasible option with the euclidean $N_A$-dimensional
norm. The term $c_\A$ is the quantity describing the energy loss by considering an a priori
\emph{ambient} reduction. \emph{Ambient} is a heuristic word, describing some determined space in which solutions
are embedded in a non-trainable fashion. Its construction can, for example, be done by a \emph{Proper Orthogonal Decomposition.}

Next, we will discuss this Proper Orthogonal Decomposition and related concepts. For instance the 
Kolmogorov $N$-width, which lets us derive many key concepts and is an important quantity in the field of ROM methods. 
We base this subsection on \cite{quarteroni2015rb}, but import the generalizations to arbitrary inner products from
\cite{franco2024}. 
\begin{definition}[Generalized Proper Orthogonal Decomposition] \label{def: POD}
    Assume $N_\text{data} < \infty$ and let $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ be a mass matrix.
    We define the time trajectory $U_j = [u(\mu_j, t_k)]_{k = 1}^{N_t} \in \R^{N_h \times N_t}$ 
    for a given $\mu_j \in \Pcal$ and consequently $U = [U_j]_{j=1}^{N_s}
    \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}}$ 
    the collective snapshot matrix. Then the \emph{(discrete) correlation matrix} is given by
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: discrete correlation matrix}
        K = \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} U \G U^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}.
    \end{equation}
    $K$ is symmetric and semi-positive definite and thus has real and 
    positive eigenvalues $\sigma^2 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r^2$. 
    Let $\psi^\mu_k$ be the corresponding eigenvectors of the correlation matrix $K$. 
    Then the POD basis of dimension $N < N_h$ is given via
    \begin{equation*}
        \xi_k = \frac{1}{\sigma_k} U \psi_k, \quad 1 \leq k \leq N,
    \end{equation*}
    for the $N$ largest eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors. Finally, stacking these eigenvectors gives us
    the \emph{POD matrix}
    \begin{equation*}
        \A := \left[\xi_k\right]_{k=1}^N \in \R^{N_h \times N}.
    \end{equation*}
    This matrix is $\G$-orthonormal by construction.
    Further, if $N_s, N_t = \infty$, we define the \emph{continuous correlation matrix} via
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: continuous correlation matrix}
        K_\infty = \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} u(\mu, t) \G u^T(\mu, t) d(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h \times N_h},
    \end{equation}
    and its real eigenvalues by $\sigma_{k, \infty}^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{Generalized Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)} \label{algo: pod}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require snapshot matrix $U = [U_j]_{j=1}^{N_s} \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}}$, 
        mass matrix $\G \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$,
        reduction $N < N_h$
        \Ensure POD basis matrix $\A^\mu = [\xi^\mu_1, \dots, \xi^\mu_N] \in \R^{N_h \times N}$
        \If{$N_\text{data} \leq N_h$}
            \State Form the correlation matrix $C = \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} U^T \G U
            \in \R^{N_\text{data} \times N_\text{data}}$
            \State Solve the eigenvalue problem $C \psi_i = \sigma_i^2 \psi_i$, \quad $i=1,\dots,r$
            \State Set $\xi_i = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_i} U \psi_i$, \quad $i=1,\dots,r$
        \Else
            \State Form the correlation matrix $K = \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} 
            U \G U^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$
            \State Solve the eigenvalue problem $K \xi_i = \sigma_i^2 \xi_i$, \quad $i=1,\dots,r$
        \EndIf
        \State $\A \gets \text{stack}(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N)$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


Following this definition, is a known legacy result, which will be among
the most used statements in Chapter (\ref{chapter: results}) of this thesis.
It shows optimality of the POD 
and relates the projection error of the Proper Orthogonal Decomposition with its eigenvalues. 
These values are attained quite easily, which is important to keep in mind. 


\begin{proposition} \label{prop: eigenvalues and optimal projection under sampling}
    Let $\mathcal{V}_N = \{ W \in \R^{N_h \times N} \, \vert \, W^T \G W = I_N \}$ be the set of all 
    $N$-dimensional $\G$-orthonormal matrices, 
    $N_\text{data} := N_t N_s$ and $\A = [\xi]_{i=1}^N$ be the POD matrix according to Definition
    (\ref{def: POD}), then
    \begin{multline*}
        \frac{\euknorm{}\Theta \times [0, T]}{N_\text{data}}
        \sum\limits_{i \in I, k = 1, \dots, N_t} \norm{u(\mu_j, t_k) - \A \A^T \G u(\mu_j, t_k)}^2 \\
        = \min\limits_{W \in \mathcal{V}_N} \frac{\euknorm{}\Theta \times [0, T]}{N_\text{data}} 
        \sum\limits_{i \in I, k = 1, \dots, N_t} 
        \norm{u(\mu_j, t_k) - W W^T \G u(\mu_j, t_k)}^2 
        = \sum_{j=N+1}^r \sigma_k^2.
    \end{multline*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[p.125--126]{quarteroni2015rb}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark} \label{remark: continuous correlation existence}
    The equivalent statement can be made for a POD matrix $\A_\infty \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ coming from 
    the continuous correlation matrix $K_\infty \in \R^{N_h \times N_h}$. See \cite{kunischvolkwein2001}.
\end{remark}


In addition, we can further explore the relation between the discrete correlation matrix and
its singular values attained as a by-product from Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod}) and the continuous 
correlation matrix defined above. For this we pose the following.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: existence and convergence to sigma infty}
    Let $K$ and $K_\infty$ and their respective eigenvalues be defined as in Definition (\ref{def: POD}), 
    then letting $N_s, N_t \to \infty$, we get 
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{k > N} \sigma_k^2 \longrightarrow \sum_{k > N} \sigma_{k, \infty}^2, \quad \mbox{ a.s.}
    \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $N_s, N_t < \infty$, conforming to the sampling assumption, and 
    \begin{equation*}
        M := 
        \underset{(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times \mathcal{T}}{\operatorname{ess}\, \sup}
        \norm{u(\mu, t)} < \infty
    \end{equation*} 
    (see introduction in this Section 
    (\ref{subsec: reduced basis framework})).
    Following Remark (\ref{remark: continuous correlation existence}), we know, that 
    there exists a $\G$-orthonormal matrix $\A_\infty \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ such that 
    \begin{align*}
        \sum_{k > N} \sigma^2_{k, \infty} &= \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} \norm{u(\mu, t) - \A_\infty \A^T_\infty \G u(\mu, t)}^2 d(\mu, t) \\
        &= \underset{W \in \R^{N_h \times N}: W^T \G W = I}{\min} \, \int_{\mu \times \mathcal{T}} \norm{u(\mu, t) - W W^T \G u(\mu, t) }^2 d(\mu, t).
    \end{align*}
    This is the optimal choice of an approximation matrix for the continuous case. However,
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ given by Definition (\ref{def: POD}) is only optimal in the discrete setting and 
    not necessarily the continuous case. Thus, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{k > N} \sigma^2_{k, \infty} \leq \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} 
        \norm{u(\mu, t) - \A \A^T \G u(\mu, t)}^2 d(\mu, t) = \sum_{k > N} \sigma^2_k.
    \end{equation*} 
    Note, that we can uniquely identify $\G^{1/2}$ as the matrix solving $\G^{1/2} \G^{1/2} = \G$, since
    it is symmetric and positive definite by definition. 
    We then write entry-wise for $m, l = 1, \dots N_h$
    \begin{align*}
        [K_\infty - K]_{ml} :=& \int_{\Theta \times [0, T]} [\G^{1/2}u(\mu, t)]_m [\G^{1/2}u(\mu, t)]_l d(\mu, t) \\
        &- \frac{\vert \Theta \times [0, T] \vert}{N_{data}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_s} 
        \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} [\G^{1/2}u(\mu_j, t_k)]_m [\G^{1/2}u(\mu_j, t_k)]_l.
    \end{align*}
    The resulting matrix is subject to the strong law of large numbers.
    This is straight forward
    by the uniform iid sampling assumed, and the general bound on $\sup_{(\mu, t)} \norm{u(\mu, t)} \leq M$ before.
    Hence, this matrix converges almost surely entry-wise to zero as 
    $N_t, N_s \to \infty$.
    
    Now using Bauer-Fike's Theorem (\ref{theo: bauer fike}) with the $\opnorm{\cdot}$-norm, we have upon ordering, 
    that for each $\sigma^2_{k, \infty}$ there is a $\sigma^2_{k}$ in the spectrum of $K$, such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \vert \sigma_k^2  - \sigma_{k, \infty}^2 \vert \leq \kappa(\A_\infty) \opnorm{K - K_\infty}.
    \end{equation*}
    Here $\kappa(\A_\infty)$ denotes the condition number of the stacked eigenvector matrix of 
    $K_\infty$, i.e. $A_\infty = (\xi_{1, \infty}, \dots, \xi_{N_h, \infty})$. This number is independent of $N_s$ and $N_t$.
    Now by convergence of $\opnorm{K - K_\infty} \to 0$ almost surely for $N_s, N_t \to \infty$, we conclude the proof. 
\end{proof}

As already hinted, Proposition (\ref{prop: eigenvalues and optimal projection under sampling}) 
can be understood as an optimality statement under a sample $i \in I, k = 1, \dots, N_t$ or even the whole space.
In combination with Proposition (\ref{prop: existence and convergence to sigma infty}), this
enables us to classify parametric partial differential equations regarding their projectability
onto a linear space. This is done using the \emph{linear Kolmogorov $N$-width} (KnW).

\begin{definition}[Linear Kolmogorov $N$-width] \label{def: linear kolmogorov}
    Let $N \in \N$, $N \leq N_h$ and $\Scal = \{ u(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h} \, 
    \vert \, (\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T] \}$ 
    be some parametric manifold. The linear Kolmogorov $N$-width of $\Scal$ is defined as
    \begin{equation*}
        d_N(\Scal) = \inf\limits_{\V \in \R^{N_h \times N}} \sup\limits_{u \in S_{N_h}} 
        \norm{u - \V \V^T \G u}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

The Kolmogorov $N$-width can generally be approximated quite well via the tail sums of
the eigenvalues of the discrete correlation matrix for large samples. 
This quantity then allows us to assert a priori,
how well any POD-based reduction method will work on the specific PPDE. Namely, if the tail sum for a choice of reduction 
dimension $N \leq N_h$ is small, then the POD retains most of the energy and a linear reduction can yield high efficiency.

On the other hand, non-linear problems, and specifically hyperbolic and transport dominated PPDEs, have
the tendency to have slowly decaying Kolmogorov $N$-width. This means they retain energy only for large choices of $N$.
Thus, the reduction must then potentially commit a non-linear mapping, motivating the following definition:

\begin{definition}[Nonlinear Kolmogorov $N$-width] \label{def: nonlinear kolmogorov}
    Let $N \in \N$, $N \leq N_h$ and $\Scal= \{ u(\mu, t) \in \R^{N_h} \, 
    \vert \, (\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T] \}$ 
    be some parametric manifold. The nonlinear Kolmogorov $N$-width of $\Scal$ is defined as 
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta_N(\Scal) =\underset{\psi \in C(\R^{N_h}, \R^N),\\ \psi' \in C(\R^N, \R^{N_h})}{\inf} 
        \sup\limits_{u \in S_{N_h}} \euknorm{u - \psi(\psi'(u))}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}





\section{Neural Networks} \label{section: background for nn}

In this section, we will introduce the general notion of neural networks using the notation
and most arguments of \cite{gühring2019nn}, if not given otherwise. Specifically important for us will be 
concatenations of dense linear layers, which are a specific subclass of networks, the so-called 
\emph{Deep Feedforward Neural Network} (DFNN).

\begin{definition}[Neural Network] \label{def: neural network}
    Let $d, L \in \N$. A \emph{neural network $\Phi$ with input dimension $d$ and $L$ layers} is defined via 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2), \dots, (A_L, b_L)),
    \end{equation*} 
    such that $N_0 = d$ and $N_1, \dots, N_L \in \N$, where each $A_l$ is an $N_l \times \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} N_k$ dimensional
    matrix, and $b_l \in \R^{N_l}$.
    
    We further define $R_\rho(\Phi): \R^d \to \R^{N_L}$ as a \emph{realization of $\Phi$ with activation function $\rho$} for some
    arbitrary map $\rho : \R \to \R$ as the result of the following scheme for some input $x \in R^d$.
    \begin{align*}
        x_0 &:= x, \\
        x_l &:= \rho \left( A_l \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c]x_0^T & \cdots & x_{l-1}^T\end{bmatrix}^T + b_l \right), \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1, \\
        x_L &:= A_L \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c]x_0^T & \cdots & x_{l-1}^T\end{bmatrix}^T + b_L,
    \end{align*}
    where $\rho$ acts entry-wise, i.e. $\rho(y) := [\rho(y_1), \dots, \rho(y_m)]^T \in \R^m$ for each 
    $y = [y_1, \dots, y_m] \in \R^m$. Note that we can write
    \begin{equation*}
        A_l = \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c]A_{l, x_0} & \cdots & A_{l, x_{l-1}}\end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation*}
    with $A_{l, x_k}$ being a $(N_l \times N_k)$-dimensional matrix for $k = 0, \dots, l-1$ and $l = 1, \dots, L$.
    This simplifies the scheme via
    \begin{align*}
        x_l &:= \rho \left( A_{l, x_0} x_0 + \dots + A_{l, x_{l-1}} x_{l-1} + b_l \right), \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1, \\
        x_L &:= A_{L, x_{L-1}} x_{L-1} + b_L.
    \end{align*}
    We call $N_{\Phi} := d + \sum_{l=1}^L N_l$ the \emph{number of neurons} of the network, $L_{\Phi} := L$
    the \emph{number of layers}, and $\omega_\Phi := \sum_{l=1}^L (\lzeronorm{A_l} + \lzeronorm{b_l})$ the \emph{number 
    of active weights}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Note that, we can rewrite known neural network architectures, such as a Convolutional Autoencoder, 
    as a neural network according to Definition (\ref{def: neural network}), see also \cite[p. 22]{gühring2020nn}.
\end{remark}

From this we can infer a simpler definition, describing a network which omits any connection of non-neighboring
layers.

\begin{definition}[Standard Neural Network] \label{def: standard neural network}
    If a neural network $\Phi = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_L, b_L))$ now has the characteristic
    \begin{equation*}
        A_{l, x_i} = 0, \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L, \mbox{ and } i = 0, \dots, l-2,
    \end{equation*}
    then we call $\Phi$ a \emph{standard neural network}.
\end{definition}
A visualization of a standard neural network can be found in Figure (\ref{fig:standard-nn}).
If we compare two distinct neural networks, with the same dimensional in- and output, $\Phi_1 = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_L, b_L))$
and $\Phi_2 = ((\hat{A}_1, \hat{b}_1), \dots, (\hat{A}_L, \hat{b}_L))$, it is implied, 
that if $[A_1]_{ij}= 0$, while $[\hat{A}_1]_{ij} \neq 0$ for some $i, j$, these networks are
severely different. But if they only differ in the value of a specific non-zero weight, they could be recovered from each
other through training. This inspires the definition of \emph{an architecture of a network}.

\begin{definition}[Neural Network Architecture] \label{def: neural network architecture}
    For some $d, L \in \N$, we define a \emph{neural network architecture $\mathcal{A}$ with input dimension $d$
    and $L$ layers} to be a binary neural network, i.e. for all entries of $A_i, b_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i = 1, \dots, L$ of $\mathcal{A}$.

    We further say that a \emph{neural network} $\Phi = ((\hat{A}_1, \hat{b}_1), \dots, (\hat{A}_L, \hat{b}_L))$ has \emph{architecture} $\mathcal{A}$, if
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $N_l(\Phi) = N_l$ for all $l = 1, \dots, L$, and
        \item $[\hat{A}_l]_{ij} \neq 0$ implies $[A_l]_{ij} \neq 0$ for all $l = 1, \dots, L$ with $i = 1, \dots, N_l$ and
        $j = 1, \dots, \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} N_k$.
    \end{enumerate} 
\end{definition}

The associated realization of a neural network $R_\rho(\Phi)$ requires the definition of $\rho$, the activation function.
Therefore, we will
define the most commonly used one in literature. It will help with some of the constructions, since it has a simple nature, 
but also provides the network with the necessary non-linearity.

\begin{definition}[ReLU] \label{def: relu activation function}
    The function
    \begin{equation*}
        \rho: \R \to \R; \quad x \mapsto \max(0, x),
    \end{equation*}
    is called \emph{ReLU} (Rectified Linear Unit) \emph{activation function.}
\end{definition}

Given such an activation function, Kurt Hornik provided in 1990 a significant contribution to the field of approximation
theory. He proved, that for any $L^p(\mu)$ function, with $\mu$ some finite measure, there is
an arbitrarily close ReLU valued neural network realization \cite{hornik1991nn}.

\begin{theorem}[Hornik's Theorem] \label{theo: hornik theorem}
    Define for some continuous, unbounded and non-constant activation function $\rho$ the set of all feedforward neural networks, 
    as
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathcal{N}_d(\rho) := \bigcup_{L=1}^\infty \bigg\{ R_\rho(\Phi): \R^d \to \R \, \big\vert \, \Phi \mbox{ is neural network with } L\mbox{ layers} \bigg\}.
    \end{equation*}
    Then $\mathcal{N}_d(\rho)$ lies dense in $L^p(\mu)$ for every finite measure $\mu$, $p \in (0, \infty)$.
\end{theorem}

The proof of this statement leverages on Hahn Banach. Thus, it does not provide the specific architecture of
the required neural network, which would be used for such an approximation. In our case, we will however focus
on an explicit construction to attain bounds for the complexity of the network used. We will hence
not mention the proof beyond this outline. 

\subsection{Network concatenations}

In this subsection we will discuss the concatenations of multiple neural networks and the effects on its characteristic
numbers. The complexity of a concatenated network can be simplified, by using the ReLU as the activation function.

\begin{definition}[Network Concatenation] \label{def: network concatenation}
    Let $L_1, L_2 \in \N$ and 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi^1 = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_{L_1}, b_{L_1})), \quad \Phi^2 = ((B_1, c_1), \dots, (B_{L_2}, c_{L_2})),
    \end{equation*}
    be two neural networks such that the input layer of $\Phi^1$ has the same dimension as the output layer of $\Phi^2$.
    Then, $\Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^2$ denotes the following $L_1 + L_2 - 1$ layered network.
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^2 := \left( ((B_1, c_1), \dots, (B_{L_2 - 1}, c_{L_2 - 1})), ((\tilde{A}_1, \tilde{b}_1), \dots, (\tilde{A}_{L_1}, \tilde{b}_{L_1})) \right),
    \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*}
        \tilde{A}_l^1 := \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c|c]A_{l, x_0} \, B_{L_2} & A_{l, x_1} & \cdots & A_{l, x_{l-1}}\end{bmatrix},
        \quad \mbox{and} \quad \tilde{b}_l^1 := A_{l, x_0} \, c_{L_2} + b_l,
    \end{equation*}
    for $l = 1, \dots, L_1$. We call $\Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^2$ the \emph{concatenation} of $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Neutral Neural Network]
    Let $\Phi^{\text{Id}} = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2))$ be a $d$-dimensional neural network, with
    \begin{equation*}
        A_1 := \begin{bmatrix} \text{Id}_{\R^d} \\ -\text{Id}_{\R^d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_1 := 0, \quad
        A_2 := \begin{bmatrix}[c|c|c] 0_{\R^{d \times d}} & \text{Id}_{\R^d} & - \text{Id}_{\R^d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_2 := 0.
    \end{equation*}
    Then $R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{Id}}) = \text{Id}_{\R^d}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: network concatenation}
    Let two neural networks $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^1$ be according to Definition (\ref{def: network concatenation}).
    Then
    $\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2 := \Phi^1 \bullet \Phi^{\text{id}} \bullet \Phi^2$ is a neural network with $d$-dimensional input, $L_1 + L_2$ layers
    with $\omega_{\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2} \leq 2\omega_{\Phi^1}
    + 2\omega_{\Phi^2}$ active weights, $N_{\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2} \leq 2N_{\Phi^1} + 2N_{\Phi^2}$ neurons. Additionally,
    for all $x \in \R^d$, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi^1 \odot \Phi^2)(x) = R_\rho(\Phi^1) \circ R_\rho(\Phi^2)(x).
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This can be proven by direct computation, see \cite{gühring2019nn}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Network parallelization}

We have shown, that it is possible to concatenate networks, such that they show the same behavior as
composed functions do. To 
further build upon that idea, we will now introduce parallelization. For this, define a network $P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)$
from two networks $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$, such that it
emulates the stacked evaluation of 
two functions, i.e. $R_\rho(P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2))(x) = (R_\rho(\Phi^1)(x), R_\rho(\Phi^2)(x))$ for all $x \in \R^d$.

\begin{definition}[Network Parallelization] \label{def: network parallelization}
    Let $L_1, L_2 \in \N$ s.t. $L_1 \leq L_2$ and let 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi^1 = ((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_{L_1}, b_{L_1})), \quad \Phi^2 = ((B_1, c_1), \dots, (B_{L_2}, c_{L_2})),
    \end{equation*}
    be two neural networks with $d$-dimensional input. We call
    \begin{equation*}
        P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2) := \left( (C_1, d_1), \dots, (C_{L_2}, d_{L_2}) \right),
    \end{equation*}
    the \emph{parallelization of $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$} with
    \begin{equation*}
        C_l := 
        \begin{bmatrix}[c|cc|c|cc]
        A_{l,x_0} & A_{l,x_1} & 0 & \cdots & A_{l,x_{l-1}} & 0 \\
        B_{l,x_0} & 0 & B_{l,x_1} & \cdots & 0 & B_{l,x_{l-1}}
        \end{bmatrix},
        \qquad
        d_l :=
        \begin{bmatrix}
        b_l \\
        c_l
        \end{bmatrix},
        \qquad \text{for } 1 \le l < L_1,
    \end{equation*}

    \begin{equation*}
        C_{l} :=
        \begin{bmatrix}[c|cc|c|cc|c|c|c]
        B_{l,x_0} & 0 & B_{l, x_1} & \cdots & 0 & B_{l, x_{L_1 - 1}} & B_{l, x_{L_1}} & \cdots & B_{l, x_{l-1}} \\
        \end{bmatrix},
        \qquad
        d_{l} := c_{l},
    \end{equation*}
    for $L_1 \leq l < L_2$, and
    \begin{equation*}
        C_{L_2} :=
        \begin{bmatrix}[c|cc|c|cc|c|c|c]
        A_{L_1,x_0} & A_{L_1,x_1} & 0 & \cdots & A_{L_1, x_{L_1 - 1}} & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\
        B_{L_2,x_0} & 0 & B_{L_2,x_1} & \cdots & 0 & B_{L_2,x_{L_1-1}} & B_{L_2, x_{L_1}} & \cdots & B_{L_2, x_{L_2 - 1}}
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*}
        d_{L_2} :=
        \begin{bmatrix}
        b_{L_1} \\
        c_{L_2}
        \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation*}

    A similar construction is used for the case $L_1 > L_2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: network parallelization}
    Let $\Phi^1$ and $\Phi^2$ be as in Definition (\ref{def: network parallelization}). Then 
    $P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)$ is a neural network with $d$-dimensional input, $\max\{L_1, L_2\}$ layers, 
    $\omega_{P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)} = \omega_{\Phi^1} + \omega_{\Phi^2}$ active weights and $N_{P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2)} = N_{\Phi^1} + N_{\Phi^2} - d$ neurons.
    Additionally, for all $x \in \R^d$, we have that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_{\rho}(P(\Phi^1, \Phi^2))(x) = (R_{\rho}(\Phi^1)(x), R_{\rho}(\Phi^2)(x)).
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This can be again shown by direct computation. See \cite{gühring2019nn}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Standard Neural Networks}

Here, we only want to briefly state an interesting fact, following from the definition of the standard networks, that
motivates the treatment of any neural network architecture as a standard one. This generally allows us to omit the 
cumbersome nature of complete connectivity.

\begin{corollary} \label{cor: eqivalence nn and standard nn}
    Let $\rho$ be the ReLU activation function, and
    $\Phi$ a neural network with $d$-dimensional input and $L = L(\Phi)$ layers, $N = N(\Phi)$
    neurons and $M = M(\Phi)$ nonzero weights. Then there is a standard neural network $\Phi_{\text{st}}$ with
    $d$-dimensional input, $L$ layers, at most $C_1 L N$ neurons and $C_2 \cdot (L N + M)$ nonzero weights, such that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi)(x) = R_\rho(\Phi_{\text{st}})(x)
    \end{equation*}
    for all $x \in \R^d$ with $C_1, C_2 > 0$ being constants.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[p.9]{gühring2019nn}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Approximation Theory}

For all following theoretical results we will assume that the activation function $\rho$ is the ReLU as given
in Definition (\ref{def: relu activation function}). Furthermore, define the set of functions bounded in Sobolev norm as
\begin{equation*}
    \Fcal_{n,d,p,B} := \big\{ f \in W^{n,p}((0,1)^d) \, \big\vert \, \snorm{f}[n][p][d] \leq B \big\}.
\end{equation*}

In this subsection, we will briefly discuss the needed steps to 
find a bound for the active weights and layers used for a neural network, approximating an arbitrary
function $f$ of some regularity. We first construct a network, that realizes an interpolated multiplication
operation. This quasi-multiplication will be able to relate a partition of unity using polynomial functions
with a neural network. For this, we need to
take the interpolation error into account, which is closely linked to the regularity of the function, we want
to approximate. For instance, if we have a
function $f$ in the Sobolev space $W^{2, 2}((0,1)^d)$, then it is apparent, that at least two weak derivatives of $f$ must exist.
Therefore, this can be leveraged to get a Taylor expansion around some selected points.

This procedure will deduce a quantifiable error of the network approximation for any $W^{1, \infty}(\Omega)$ function.

This leaves us with two main objectives.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bound the Sobolev function at critical equidistant points, using its Taylor expansion.
    \item Relate a fixed error rate of the approximation polynomial and some neural network to the networks complexity.
\end{enumerate}

These two objectives are pursued by both Yarotsky \cite{yarotsky2017nn} and Gühring et al \cite{gühring2019nn}.
Note, that Yarotsky
only provides the special case $s=0, B=1$, since the proof only shows an estimation with a Taylor expansion
regarding the $L^\infty$ norm. This misses the crucial consideration of the weak derivative bound for Lipschitz functions.


Even Though we will use the statement of
Gühring later on, which is the extension of Yarotsky to any choice of $s, p$ and $B$, 
we will only give the idea of 
the proof for Gühring. It is similar to the proof of Yarotsky, but does include a lot of further 
theoretical setting, which will not be important for the actual results presented in this paper.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: approximation by square}
    Let $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$ and $f: [0, 1] \to [0, 1]; \,  x \mapsto x^2$. Then
    there are constants $c_1, c_2, c_3 > 0$, such that there is a
    neural network $\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon$ with $1$-dimensional
    input and output, realizing
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon) - f}_{L^\infty((0, 1))}\leq \varepsilon,
    \end{equation*}
    and $R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon)(0) = 0$. This network has at most 
    $c_1 \cdot \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ nonzero weights, at most
    $c_2 \cdot \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ layers, at most $c_3 \cdot \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ neurons.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Define the \emph{tooth function} $g: [0,1] \to [0,1]$ by
    \begin{equation*}
        g(x) = \begin{cases}
            2x, \quad &\text{for } x < 1/2, \\
            2(1-x), \quad &\text{for } x \geq 1/2,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    and its composition, the \emph{sabertooth function}, 
    \begin{equation*}
        g_s(x) := \underbrace{g \circ g \circ \dots \circ g(x)}_{s \text{-times}}. 
    \end{equation*}
    It has been proven in \cite{telgarsky2015}, that $g_s$ then has the following
    explicit representation
    \begin{equation*}
        g_s(x) = \begin{cases}
            2^s\left( x - \frac{2k}{2^s} \right), \quad &\text{for } x \in \left[\frac{2k}{2^s}, \frac{2k + 1}{2^s}\right], 
            k = 0, \dots 2^{s-1}-1, \\[0.8em]
            2^s\left(\frac{2k}{2^s} - x \right), \quad &\text{for } x \in \left[\frac{2k - 1}{2^s}, \frac{2k}{2^s}\right], 
            k = 1, \dots 2^{s-1}. \\
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    Furthermore, from the definition of the ReLU function (refer to Definition (\ref{def: relu activation function})),
    we can also set $g(x) = 2 \rho(x) - 4 \rho(x - 1/2) + 2 \rho(x - 1)$. This is the same as defining
    a neural network with $1$-dimensional input and output via $\Phi = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2))$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        A_1 = \begin{bmatrix}
            \text{Id}_\R \\ \text{Id}_\R \\ \text{Id}_\R
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_1 = \begin{pmatrix}
            0 \\ -1/2 \\ -1
        \end{pmatrix}, \quad
        A_2 = \begin{bmatrix}
            2 & -4 & 2 
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_2 = 0.
    \end{equation*}
    Then it is evident, using Lemma (\ref{lemma: network concatenation}), that $g_m(x) = R_\rho(\overbrace{\Phi \odot \dots \odot \Phi}^{m \text{-times}})(x)$ 
    for all $x \in [0, 1]$ represents a network with $\Ocal(m)$ layers, active weights and neurons. 
    Now, we set up a linear combination of these sabertooth functions as 
    \begin{align*}
        f_m(x) &:= x - \sum_{s=1}^m \frac{g_s(x)}{2^{2s}} \\
        &= \frac{2k+1}{2^m} x - \frac{k(k+1)}{2^{2m}}, \quad \text{for } x \in \left[\frac{k}{2^m}, \frac{k+1}{2^m}\right], 
        k = 0, 1, \dots, 2^m - 1,
    \end{align*}
    and any $m \in \N$. In fact, if we define the dyadic intervals $I_k := [\frac{k}{2^m}, \frac{k+1}{2^m}]$ like above, 
    then we can argue with the slope and the value separately. Let $m \in \N$ be fixed and $k = 0, 1, \dots, 2^m - 1$. If 
    \begin{equation*}
        x = \frac{k + \alpha}{2^m}, \quad \text{with } \alpha \in [0, 1),
    \end{equation*}
    we can assure $0 \leq \theta/2^{m-s} < 1$ for any $s \leq m$. Therefore, the slope is constant on each $I_k$. 
    And since the slope for each segment of a fixed sabertooth function $g_s$ is the same in magnitude,
    we write
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{d}{dx} f_m(x) = 1 - \sum_{s=1}^m \frac{g_s'(x)}{2^{2s}} = 1 - \sum_{s=1}^m \sigma_s(x) 2^{-s},
    \end{equation*}
    for any $x \in \mathring{I_k}$. Here, for $q_s = \lfloor k/2^m \rfloor$, the lower interval boundary, we define
    \begin{equation*}
        \sigma_s(x) = \begin{cases}
            +1, \quad &\text{if }q_s \text{ is even,}\\
            -1, \quad &\text{if }q_s \text{ is odd.}
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    Notice, that we can identify any natural number $k \leq m$ with a binary representation
    \begin{equation*}
        k = \sum_{r=0}^{m-1} b_r 2^{r}, \quad \text{with } b_r \in \{0, 1\}.
    \end{equation*}
    This is useful, because we know with boolean arithmetics, that if the first digit is zero, the natural number
    it represent must be even. Thus write
    \begin{equation*}
        q_s = \sum_{r = m-s}^{m-1} b_r 2^{r-(m-s)},
    \end{equation*}
    which lets us denote the parity of $q_s$ with $b_{m-s}$, the first digit. Now, writing $\varepsilon_s := 1/2(1 - \sigma_s) \in \{0, 1\}$
    on any $I_k$, we get for the total slope for $x \in \mathring{I_k}$
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{d}{dx} f_m(x) &= 1 - \sum_{s=1}^{m} \sigma_s(x) 2^{-s} \\
        &= 1 - \sum_{s=1}^{m} 2^{-s} + 2 \sum_{s=1}^{m} \varepsilon_s(x) 2^{-s} \\
        &= 1 - (1 - 2^{-m}) + 2 \sum_{s=1}^m b_{m-s} 2^{-s} \\
        &= 2^{-m}+ 2 \sum_{r=0}^{m-1} b_r 2^{-m+r} = 2^{-m} + 2 \cdot 2^{-m} k.
    \end{align*}
    Further, for the shifts, because we know this function is piecewise affine, 
    we can simply solve for $c_k \in \R$ on each $I_k$
    \begin{equation*}
        f_m\left(\frac{k}{2^m}\right) = \frac{2k+1}{2^m} \cdot \frac{k}{2^m} + \alpha_k = \left(\frac{k}{2^m}\right)^2.
    \end{equation*}

    Now, since we know that the function $f_m$ is just an addition of at most $m$ layered networks
    \begin{equation*}
        g_m(x) = g \circ \cdots \circ g (x) = R_\rho(\Phi \odot \cdots \odot \Phi),    
    \end{equation*} 
    and the input, 
    we can set up a neural network realizing $f_m$. This is done straight forward using a layer, which adds
    all layers together, while inverting each of the non-inputs. We call the subsequent neural network 
    $\Phi^\text{sq}_m$. This network then has $\Ocal(m + 1)$ layers, non-zero weights and neurons.
      
    Finally, we can use the explicit definition of $f_m = R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_m)$ for some fixed $m \in \N$
    to attain an $L^\infty$-approximation bound. For this, simply use a piece-wise extrema discussion on
    each $\mathring{I_k}$, for $k = 0,1, \dots, 2^m -1$. We derive
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{d}{dx}\left(f_m(x) - f(x)\right) = \frac{d}{dx}\left( 
            \frac{2k+1}{2^m} x - \frac{k(k+1)}{2^m} - x^2
         \right) = \frac{2k+1}{2^m} - 2x.
    \end{equation*}
    Setting this to zero, and inserting it in the antiderivative gives us for each $I_k$ a $k$-independent bound of
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_m) - f}_{L^\infty((0,1))} = \norm{f_m - f}_{L^\infty((0,1))} = 2^{-2m-2}.
    \end{equation*}
    Hence, setting $m = \ceil{\ln(1/\varepsilon)}$, with $C, C' > 0$ constants, only dependent on
    the domain, we achieve the wanted bound from the statement for the neural network
    $\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\varepsilon := \Phi^\text{sq}_m$ with $\Ocal(m) = 
    \Ocal(\ln(1 / \varepsilon) + 1) \leq \Ocal(2 \ln(1 / \varepsilon))$
    layers, neurons and active weights.
\end{proof}

This statement enables us to express multiplication using a neural network. This can be shown
by considering the polarization identity
\begin{equation} \label{eq: polarization identify}
    xy = \frac{1}{2}((x + y)^2 - x^2 - y^2), \quad \text{for } x,y \in \R, 
\end{equation}
as given by the first binomial formula.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: quasi multiplication}
    Let $M > 0$ and $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$. Then there is a ReLU neural network $\tilde{\times}$ with $2$-dimensional input and
    $1$-dimensional output, and constants $c_1 > 0$ and $c_2 = c_2(M) > 0$, such that for $\times: \R \times \R \to \R; (x, y) \mapsto xy$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\norm{R_\rho(\tilde{\times})- \times}_{W^{0, \infty}((-M, M)^2)} \leq \varepsilon$,
        \item if $x = 0$ or $y = 0$, then $R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y) = 0$,
        \item the number of layers, active weights and neurons of $\tilde{\times}$ is at most $c_1 \ln(1/\varepsilon) + c_2$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\delta := \varepsilon/(6M^2)$. Then we construct
    an approximation of the square function $f(x) = x^2$ for $x \in (0, 1)$ 
    using Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}) for the chosen $\delta$. Indeed, we define $\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\delta$,
    such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{R_\rho(\Phi^{\text{sq}}_\delta) - f}[0][\infty][1] \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{6M^2},
    \end{equation*}
    which now of course has $\Ocal(\ln(1/\delta))$ many layers, active weights and neurons. To achieve 
    this we have to implement negative numbers as well. With $\vert x \vert = \rho(x) + \rho(-x)$, it can be shown,
    that it is indeed possible to expand any neural network with a positive domain to a negative domain using a neural network (by simply concatenating).
    Thus, we construct $\tilde{\times}$ via appending an 
    addition layer on top of parallel square networks with scaled input, achieving
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y) = 2 M^2 \left( R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) 
        \left( \frac{\vert x + y \vert}{2M} \right) - 
        R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) \left( \frac{\vert x \vert}{2M} \right)
        - R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) \left( \frac{\vert  y \vert}{2M} \right) \right).
    \end{equation*}
    We need the third statement of our Proposition, 
    when putting together the necessary complexity of this network.
    This uses the constants in Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}) as follows.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item We have $2$ layers for the implementation of the absolute value, $1$ for the addition in the end, 
        and $c_0 \ln(1 / \delta)$ for the square function. Thus,
        $L_{\tilde{\times}} \leq c_0\ln(1/\varepsilon) + c_0 \ln(6M^2) + 3$ in total.
        \item We need $4 + 2 + 2$ active weights for the first matrix to distribute each $(x,y)$ onto the parallel networks 
        together with $6$ active weights for the superposing of the input into the $\vert \cdot \vert$-function. 
        Another $3$ are required for the
        last layer adding all outputs. Each
        of the different computations for the artificial scaled square function must be in parallel. Hence, we have
        $\omega_{\tilde{\times}} \leq 3c_0\ln(1/\varepsilon) + 3c_0\ln(6M^2) + 17$ in total.
        \item Similar to (2.), we get $9 = (2 + 1) \cdot 3$ neurons for the first two and the last layer, thus giving us
        $N_{\tilde{\times}} \leq 3c_0\ln(1/\varepsilon) + 3c_0\ln(6M^2) + 9$ in total.
    \end{enumerate}
    Note, that $c_0$ does not depend on $M$. By taking the adequate maximum, we get the desired bound on the 
    complexity in (3.).

    To make the true multiplication match the form of the implemented network, using (\ref{eq: polarization identify}),
    we may write for $x, y \in (-M, M)$
    \begin{align*}
        xy &= 4M^2 \left(\frac{x}{2M} \cdot \frac{y}{2M} \right) \\
        &= 4M^2 \frac{1}{2} \left( \left( \frac{x}{2M} + \frac{y}{2M} \right)^2 - \left( \frac{x^2}{2M} \right)^2
        \left( \frac{y^2}{2M} \right)^2 \right) \\
        &= 2M^2 \left( \left(\frac{\vert x + y \vert}{2M} \right)^2 - \left( \frac{\vert x \vert}{2M} \right)^2
        - \left( \frac{\vert y \vert}{2M} \right)^2 \right).
    \end{align*}
    Now with the deviation from each of the square approximation we obtain,
    \begin{align*}
        \norm{R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) - \times}_{L^\infty((-M, M)^2)} 
        &\leq 2M^2 \left( 3 \cdot \sup\limits_{\frac{\vert x \vert}{2M}\, : \, x \in (-M, M)}\vert \, R_\rho(\Phi^\text{sq}_\delta) 
        (\tilde{x}) - \tilde{x}^2 \, \vert \right) \\
        &\leq 6M^2 \delta = \varepsilon.
    \end{align*}
    We discuss a few things happening here for clarity. It is possible to simply use the reverse triangle equality
    to get all three error terms added, and then put them together. This can be done, since the $L^\infty((0,1)^2)$ norm 
    detects only the maximal value of the error across all possible elements of the $(0,1)^2$ domain. Here
    the inner function is bijective, so these are just the same. Additionally, we use the statement
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{g \circ f}_{L^\infty((0,1))} \leq \norm{g}_{L^\infty((0,1))}.
    \end{equation*}
    This proves the statement (1.).

    Statement (2.) 
    on the other hand, is a direct consequence of the subliminal result in Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}).
\end{proof}

As a last step, before stating and proving the Yarotsky Theorem, we will discuss the construction and complexity
of a network approximating any collection of equidistantly spaced function with local support in a higher dimensional
domain $(0,1)^d$ for $1 \leq d < \infty$. Remark, that we set up these networks, such that their realizations 
form a partition of unity on the $d$-dimensional domain. Then this construction 
will be enough to get an approximation under a corresponding complexity discussion.

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma: partition of unity}
    For any $d, N \in \N$ there is a collection of functions
    \begin{equation*}
        \Psi = \left\{ \phi_{\vec{m}} \, \vert \, \vec{m} \in \{ 0, \dots, N \}^d \right\},
    \end{equation*}
    with $\phi_{\vec{m}}: \R^d \to \R$ for all $\vec{m} = (m_1, \dots, m_d) \in \{ 0, \dots, N \}^d$ with the following properties.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $0 \leq \phi_{\vec{m}}(x) \leq 1$ for all $\phi_{\vec{m}} \in \Psi$ and every $x \in \R^d$,
        \item $\sum_{\phi_{\vec{m}} \in \Psi} \phi_{\vec{m}}(x) = 1$ for every $x \in [0,1]^d$,
        \item $\text{supp}(\phi_{\vec{m}}) \subset B_{1/N, \norm{\cdot}_{\ell^\infty}}(\vec{m} / N)$ for all $\phi_{\vec{m}} \in \Psi$,
        \item There are constants $C, c \geq 1$ such that for each $\phi_{\vec{m}} \in \Psi$ there is a neural network
        $\Phi_{\vec{m}}$ with $d$-dimensional input and output, at most $3$ layers, $C \cdot d$ active weights and neurons,
        such that
        \begin{equation*}
            \prod_{l=1}^d [R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}})]_l = \phi_{\vec{m}}
        \end{equation*}
        and $\norm{[R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}})]_l}_{L^\infty((0,1)^d)} \leq 1$ for all $l = 1, \dots, d$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    In this proof, we will construct adequate functions, which are
    easily transferable into ReLU neural networks. For this, define
    \begin{equation*}
        \psi: \R \to [0, 1], \quad \psi(x) = \begin{cases}
            1, \quad &\vert x \vert < 1, \\
            0, \quad &2 < \vert x \vert, \\
            2 - \vert x \vert \quad &1 \leq \vert x \vert \leq 2,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    and $\phi_{\vec{m}}: \R^d \to \R$ as the product of shifted and scaled versions of $\psi$, which inherit its local
    support, i.e.
    \begin{equation*}
        \phi_{\vec{m}}(x) := \prod_{l=1}^d \psi \left( 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right) \right),
    \end{equation*}
    for $\vec{m} = (m_1, \dots, m_d) \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d$ and $x = (x_1, \dots, x_d) \in \R^d$. 
    This function satisfies the properties (1.), (2.) and (3.) directly. In fact, let $x_i \in \R$ arbitrary 
    for some $i = 1, \dots, d$.
    Now we get that $\psi(3N(x_i - (m_i/N))) \in [-2, 2]$, since $\psi$ has support of $[-2, 2]$. This gives us
    \begin{equation*}
        x_i \in \left[\frac{m_i}{N} - \frac{2}{3N}, \frac{m_i}{N} + \frac{2}{3N}\right],
    \end{equation*}
    yielding the property (3.), as we took $i$ arbitrarily. Simultaneously, we use the zero product law,
    and the fact, that the range of $\psi$ is $[0, 1]$ to get property (1.). To prove property (2.) let $z_i := 3Nx_i$. Then we
    can rewrite for any $N$ the one-dimensional function 
    \begin{equation*}
        S_i(x_i) := \sum_{m_i = 0}^N \psi\left( 3N \left( x_i - \frac{m_i}{N} \right) \right)\quad \text{into the function} \quad
        \tilde{S}_i(z_i) := \sum_{m_i=0}^N \psi(z_i - 3m_i).
    \end{equation*}
    In turn, this gives us the desired result of $\tilde{S}_i(z_i) = 1$ for any $z_i \in [0, 3N]$. This is due to the
    distance between shifts to the input being $3$. This means 
    going forwards or backwards by more than that lands one in the same situation. 
    Hence, we only consider two cases without loss of generality. If $z_i \in [-1, 1]$, we directly get $1$.
    For $z_i \in [1, 2]$, we get
    \begin{equation*}
        \psi(z_i) + \psi(z_i - 3) = (2 - z_1) + (2 + (z_i - 3)) = 1.
    \end{equation*}
    We visualize this part of the proof in Figure (\ref{fig:psi-shifts}).
    Now we can extrapolate this result to $S_i$ by a simple scaling argument. Afterwards, we rearrange
    and use the factorization of the sum over products to get
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \phi_{\vec{m}}(x) = \sum_{m_1 = 0}^N \cdots \sum_{m_d = 0}^N \prod_{l=1}^d
        \psi\left( 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right) \right) = \prod_{l=1}^d \left( \sum_{m_l = 0}^N
        \psi\left( 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right) \right) \right) = 1.
    \end{equation*}

    Finally, it only remains to prove property (4.). For this we need to construct a neural network 
    $\Phi_\psi$, that realizes $\psi$. Let
    \begin{equation*}
        A_1 := \begin{bmatrix}
            1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_1 := \begin{pmatrix}
            2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \\ -2
        \end{pmatrix}, \quad
        A_2 := \begin{bmatrix}[c|cccc]
            0 & 1 & -1 & -1 & 1
        \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        b_2 := 0,
    \end{equation*}
    define the neural network $\Phi_\psi = ((A_1, b_1), (A_2, b_2))$. Then, by using the identity $\euknorm{x} = \rho(x)+\rho(-x)$, 
    it can be shown, that for all $x \in \R$
    \begin{align*}
        R_\rho(\Phi_\psi)(x) &= A_2(\rho(A_1x + b_1)) + b_2 \\
        &= A_2\left(\begin{bmatrix}
            \rho(x + 2) & \rho(x + 1) & \rho(x - 1) & \rho(x - 2)
        \end{bmatrix}^T\right) \\
        &= \rho(x + 2) + \rho(x - 2) - (\rho(x + 1) + \rho(x - 1)) \\
        &= \psi(x).
    \end{align*}
    The complexity of this network is $2$ layers, $12$ active weights and $6$ neurons.
    Let $\Phi_{\vec{m}, l}$ be a neural network with $d$-dimensional input and one-dimensional output, such that
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}, l})(x) = 3N \left( x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right), 
        \quad \text{for all } x \in \R^d \text{ and } \vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d.
    \end{equation*}
    Then the complexity of this network is $1$ layer, $2$ active weights and $d+1$ neurons. Finally, realizing
    that the composition of both these functions, i.e. the realization of the concatenation of networks, would yield
    each factor for the desired function. If we now parallelize this concatenation, we get property (4.)
    by definition. In fact, let 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi_{\vec{m}} := P(\Phi_\psi \odot \Phi_{\vec{m}, l} \, \vert \, l = 1, \dots, d),
    \end{equation*}
    since then
    \begin{equation*}
        \prod_{l=1}^d [R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}})]_l(x) = \phi_{\vec{m}}(x), \quad \text{for all }x \in \R^d.
    \end{equation*}
    This network now has $d$-dimensional input and output and by Lemma (\ref{lemma: network concatenation}) and Lemma (\ref{lemma: network parallelization}),
    has $3$ layers, at most $(12 + 2) \cdot 2 \cdot d = 28 d$ active weights, and at most $9d$ neurons.
\end{proof}

With these auxiliary results, we may finally state and proof the Yarotsky Theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Yarotsky Theorem] \label{theo: yarotsky}
    Let $d \in \N, n \in \N, B = 1$ and $f \in \Fcal_{n ,d , \infty, B}$ some arbitrary function.
    Then there is a constant $c = c(d, n) > 0$ with the following properties:
    
    For any $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$, there is a neural network architecture 
    $\Acal_\varepsilon = \Acal_\varepsilon(d, n, \varepsilon)$ with $d$-dimensional input and one-dimensional output such
    that, there is a neural network $\Phi_\varepsilon^f$ that has architecture $\Acal_\varepsilon$ and satisfies
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{f - R_\rho(\Phi_\varepsilon^f)}[0][\infty][d] \leq \varepsilon,
    \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $L_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \ln(1 / \varepsilon)$,
        \item $\omega_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$,
        \item $N_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\phi_{\vec{m}} \in \Psi$ be as constructed in the 
    proof of Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}). We will divide the proof of this theorem into
    two parts.
    First, we will formulate an approximate function $f_1$ for $f$ using the partition of unity and
    an adequate Taylor expansion. Secondly, we will find
    the subsequent approximation of that function $f_1$ by a neural network $\Phi_{f_1}$
    using the $\tilde{\times}$-multiplication of Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}).

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \noindent\textit{Step 1: Taylor Expansion}
    
    For any $\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d$, consider the $(n-1)$-Taylor polynomial for the function $f$ at
    $x_0 = \vec{m}/N$:
    \begin{equation*}
        P_{\vec{m}}(x) = \sum_{\vec{n}: \, \vert \vec{n} \vert < n} \frac{D^{\vec{n}} f(x)}{\vec{n}!} 
        \bigg\vert_{x = \frac{\vec{m}}{N}} \left( x - \frac{\vec{m}}{N} \right)^{\vec{n}},
        \quad \text{for } x \in \R^d,
    \end{equation*}
    with the conventions $\vec{n}! = \prod_{l=1}^d n_l!$ and $(x - \vec{m}/N)^{\vec{n}} = \prod_{l=1}^d(x_l - \frac{m_l}{N})^{n_l}$.
    With the partition of unity of Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}), we can then define any function,
    as a linear combination of its point-values and the partition function. In particular, this allows us
    to define
    \begin{equation*}
        f = \sum_{\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \phi_{\vec{m}} f, \quad \text{and} \quad f_1 = \sum_{\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \phi_{\vec{m}} P_{\vec{m}}.
    \end{equation*}
    Now we can utilize the properties given by Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}), as follows;
    firstly use the $\inftynorm{\phi_{\vec{m}}} = 1$ property in Step (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 1}). Then one can estimate the 
    maximal number of partitions with $2^d$ in Step (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 2}). Afterwards, we take the
    simple Lagrange remainder in Step (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 3}).
    Lastly we argue with $f \in \Fcal_{n, d, \infty, B=1}$ in Step (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 4}).
    \begin{align}
        \inftynorm{f - f_1} &\leq \sum_{\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} 
        \inftynorm{\phi_{\vec{m}} \left( f - P_{\vec{m}} \right)} \\
        &\leq \sum_{\vec{m}: \left\vert x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right\vert < \frac{1}{N}, \, \forall l = 1, \dots, d} 
        \inftynorm{f - P_{\vec{m}}} \label{align: f-f1 eq 1}\\
        &\leq 2^d \max\limits_{\vec{m}: \left\vert x_l - \frac{m_l}{N} \right\vert < \frac{1}{N}, \, \forall l = 1, \dots, d}
        \inftynorm{f - P_{\vec{m}}} \label{align: f-f1 eq 2}\\
        &\leq \frac{2^d d^n}{n!} \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^n \, 
        \max\limits_{\vec{n}: \, \vert \vec{n} \vert = n} \inftynorm{D^{\vec{n}} f} 
        \label{align: f-f1 eq 3}\\
        &\leq \frac{2^d d^n}{n!} \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^{\vec{n}} \label{align: f-f1 eq 4}
    \end{align}
    Now taking
    \begin{equation*}
        N = \ceil{\left( \frac{n!}{2^d d^n} \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \right)^{-\frac{1}{n}}}
    \end{equation*}
    gives the approximation bound of 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: f-f1 bound in yarotsky}
        \inftynorm{f - f_1} \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
    \end{equation}

    \vspace{0.5cm}
    \noindent\textit{Step 2: Approximation by a Neural Network}

    The polynomial function $f_1$ can be written out to underline its construction, which only utilized 
    the functions $\phi_{\vec{m}}$ and multiplications. In fact, 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: yarotsky f_1 def}
        f_1(x) = \sum_{\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} \sum_{\vec{n}: \, \vert \vec{n} \vert < n} a_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}} 
        \underbrace{\left( \phi_{\vec{m}}(x) \left(x - \frac{\vec{m}}{N}\right)^{\vec{n}} \right)}_{=: f_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}(x)},
    \end{equation}
    for all $x \in \R^d$.
    This expansion has at most $d^n (N + 1)^d$ terms containing $f_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}$, since 
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\vert\left\{ \vec{n} \in \{0, \dots, n - 1\}^d \, : \, \vert \vec{n} \vert < n\right\}\right\vert \\
        \leq \left\vert\left\{ \vec{n} \in \{0, \dots, n - 1\}^d \right\}\right\vert = d^n,
    \end{equation*}
    and obviously $\vert\{\vec{m} = \{0, \dots, N\}^d \}\vert = (N + 1)^d$. Note, that we take $M = d + n$ to define 
    the network multiplication, i.e. $\tilde{\times}: (-M, M)^2 \to \R$. This is done, because 
    if $x, y \in (-1, 1)$, then
    \begin{equation*}
        \vert \, R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y) - x y \, \vert \leq \delta \quad
        \implies \quad
        \vert \, R_\rho(\tilde{\times})(x, y)\, \vert \leq \vert y \vert + \delta,
    \end{equation*}
    for some fixed but not yet specified $\delta \in (0,1)$. Recursively concatenating 
    $\tilde{\times}$ for $(d + n - 1)$-times can then propagate the error to at most $M = (d + n)$ 
    upon the last realization of $\tilde{\times}$.
    
    As aforementioned, we can now infer a neural network, that has a comparable realization to the
    function $f_{\vec{m},\vec{n}}$. This network will be a concatenation of the quasi-multiplication networks $\tilde{\times}$.
    It uses $(n - 1)$ multiplications for the estimated realization of $\prod_{l=1}^d (x_l - (m_l / N))^{n_l}$, since 
    we know that $\sum_{l=1}^d n_l < n$. Additionally,
    $d$ multiplications for the estimated realization
    of the function $\phi_{\vec{m}}$ and one multiplication to put both together. Hence, we define a neural
    network $\Phi^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}_{f_1}$ with $d$-dimensional input and one dimensional output via
    \begin{multline*}
        R_\rho(\Phi^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}_{f_1})(x) = R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\bigg( [R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}})]_1(x_1), 
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\bigg([R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}})]_2(x_2), \dots \\
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) \bigg([R_\rho(\Phi_{\vec{m}})]_d(x_d), R_\rho(\tilde{\times})
        \bigg(x_1 - \frac{m_1}{N}, \dots \\
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) \bigg( x_{d-1} - \frac{m_{d-1}}{N}, R_\rho(\tilde{\times})\bigg(
        x_d - \frac{m_d}{N}, \dots \\
        R_\rho(\tilde{\times}) \bigg(x_d - \frac{m_d}{N},  x_d - \frac{m_d}{N}\bigg)
        \bigg)\bigg)\bigg)\bigg) \bigg)\bigg),
    \end{multline*}
    for $x = (x_1, \dots, x_d) \in \R^d$. Here we used $\Phi_{\vec{m}}$ as in the proof of Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}).
    This network contains at most $(d + n)$ concatenations of $\tilde{\times}$.
    Thus, by Lemma (\ref{lemma: partition of unity}), Lemma (\ref{lemma: network concatenation}),
    Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}) and the constants $C, c_0, c_1(d, n) > 0$ as mentioned, we obtain
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: yarotsky complexity with delta}
        L_{\Phi^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}_{f_1}} \leq 3 c_0 (d + n) \left(\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right) + (d + n) c_1 \right), \quad
        \omega_{\Phi^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}_{f_1}} \leq C 2(d + n) d \left(\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right) + 2(d + n) C \right),
    \end{equation}
    for an a priori fixed precision level $\delta \in (0, 1)$. Note, that the number of neurons are of the same order as 
    the number of active weights.

    The error comparison of $R_\rho(\Phi^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}_{f_1})$ and $f_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}$ can then be done by counting the maximal depth of 
    usage of the network $\tilde{\times}$,
    which is $(d + n)$ as shown above, and the estimate of 
    \begin{equation*}
        \bigg\vert \, \left(x - \frac{\vec{m}}{N}\right) \, \bigg\vert \leq 1, \quad 
        \vert \, \psi(x) \, \vert \leq 1, 
    \end{equation*} 
    for all $x \in (0, 1)^d$. Then it follows directly, that
    \begin{equation*}
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}) - f_{\vec{m},\vec{n}}} \leq (d + n) \delta.
    \end{equation*}
    As mentioned below Equation (\ref{eq: yarotsky f_1 def}), $f_1$ has at most $d^n (N + 1)^d$
    terms. But since any $x \in \R^d$ can be only in the support of at most $2^d$ functions $\phi_{\vec{m}}$ by the
    second statement in Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}), the
    sum going over all $\vec{m}$ can be reduced to only those, which are non-zero. We employ this argument in the 
    upcoming Step (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 1}). This improves the amount of summands from
    $(N+1)^d$ to $2^d$, which we will use in Step (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 2}). Setting
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{2 \cdot 2^{d} d^n(d + n)}
    \end{equation*}
    allows us to finally assert, using the bound of Equation (\ref{eq: yarotsky complexity with delta})
    in Step (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 3}), that
    \begin{align}
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}) - f_1}
        &\leq \sum_{\vec{m} \in \{0, \dots, N\}^d} 
        \sum_{\vec{n}: \, \vert \vec{n} \vert < n} \vert a_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}  \vert \,
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}) - f_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}} \\
        &= \sum_{\vec{m}: \, x \in \operatorname{supp}(\phi_{\vec{m}})} 
        \sum_{\vec{n}: \, \vert \vec{n} \vert < n} \vert a_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}} \vert \,
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}) - f_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}} \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 1}\\
        &\leq 2^d d^n \max\limits_{\vec{m}: \, x \in \operatorname{supp}(\phi_{\vec{m}})} \max\limits_{\vec{n}: \, \vert \vec{n} \vert < n}
        \inftynorm{R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1}^{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}) - f_{\vec{m}, \vec{n}}} \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 2}\\
        &\leq 2^d d^n (d + n) \delta \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 3} \\
        &= \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \label{align: yarotsky r-f1 4}
    \end{align}
    Given this $\delta$, there are at most $d^n (N + 1)^d = \Ocal(\varepsilon^{-d/n})$ parallel networks 
    with one additive layer. Moreover, the fact that
    generally we have $c'\ln(1 / \delta + c'') \leq c''' \ln(1 / \delta)$ 
    for $c', c'' > 0$ and $c''' \geq c' \ln(1 / c'')$, gets us the final complexity of $\Phi_{f_1}$ by
    Equation (\ref{eq: yarotsky complexity with delta}). In fact,
    \begin{equation*}
        L_{\Phi_{f_1}} \leq c \ln(1 / \varepsilon), \quad \omega_{\Phi_{f_1}} \leq c \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon),
        \quad N_{\Phi_{f_1}} \leq c \varepsilon^{-d/n} \ln(1/\varepsilon),
    \end{equation*}
    with some constant $c = c(d, n) > 0$.

    Using the triangle inequality, we can show that this network indeed satisfies the wanted precision bound with
    both errors as in the last Step (\ref{align: f-f1 eq 4}) and the last Step (\ref{align: yarotsky r-f1 4}), respectively:
    \begin{equation*}
        \inftynorm{f - R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1})} \leq \inftynorm{f - f_1} + \inftynorm{f_1 - R_\rho(\Phi_{f_1})}
        \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

We may extend Yarotsky to cover a more general case, where $f \in \Fcal_{n, d, p, B}$ for arbitrarily
$1 \leq p \leq \infty$ and $B > 0$ and the norm, to which we assert the precision, is an arbitrary Sobolev norm.
As a matter of fact, we will denote, that we use Yarotsky, even if $B \neq 0$, to clearly separate the
extension to the more general Sobolev-Slobodeckij norm, as long as the other prerequisites are met.
This extension is presented by Gühring et al. \cite{gühring2019nn}.

\begin{theorem}[Gühring Theorem] \label{theo: gühring}
    Let $d \in \N, n \in \N_{\geq 2}, B > 0, 1 \leq p \leq \infty$ 
    and $0 \leq s \leq 1$ and $f \in \Fcal_{n ,d , p, B}$ some arbitrary function.
    Then there is a constant $c = c(d, n, p, B, s) > 0$ with the following properties:
    
    For any $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$, there is a neural network architecture 
    $\Acal_\varepsilon = \Acal_\varepsilon(d, n, p, B, s, \varepsilon)$ with $d$-dimensional input and one-dimensional output such
    that, there is a neural network $\Phi_\varepsilon^f$ that has architecture $\Acal_\varepsilon$ and satisfies
    \begin{equation*}
        \snorm{f - R_\rho(\Phi_\varepsilon^f)}[s][p][d] \leq \varepsilon,
    \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $L_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \ln(1 / \varepsilon)$,
        \item $\omega_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/(n-s)} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$,
        \item $N_{\Acal_\varepsilon} \leq c \cdot \varepsilon^{-d/(n-s)} \ln(1/\varepsilon)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof Sketch]
    We will only sketch the general steps of the proof.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The first step is to expand the Proposition (\ref{prop: approximation by square}) to the 
        $W^{1, \infty}(0,1)$-norm. This is done by straight forward calculation.
        \item This can be applied to Proposition (\ref{prop: quasi multiplication}) in the same way. Additionally,
        we need to bound $\euknorm{R_\rho(\tilde{\times})}_{W^{1, \infty}((0,1)^2)}$, in order to
        estimate the collective error under the Taylor expanded function.
        \item The crucial next step is to prepare the switch of norms using
        \begin{equation*}
            \snorm{f - f_N}[s][p][d] \leq C \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^{n-s} \snorm{f}[n][p][d],
        \end{equation*}
        for a partition of unity of size $N$. It is worth to note, that the factor in front influences
        the choice of $\delta$ and thus is responsible for the
        different approximation in regard to weights and neurons.
        \item The last step, is
        the approximation of a sum of localized polynomials with a neural network. This requires control of the derivatives.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}




\subsection{Statistical Learning} \label{subsec: statistical learning}




We have now introduced the framework for analytical discussion regarding complexity and potential of neural networks.
It still remains to achieve a practical neural network $\Phi$ fitting the 
function in question for some fixed
architecture $\Acal_\Phi$. This crucial step in the process of 
development is referred to as \emph{module training} or \emph{statistical learning}.

In order to quantify the "falseness" of a potential classification, we need a function, that compares a true solution
to an output of the network. The following two definitions are 
based on \cite{ciampiconi2024}, where the interested reader
can find extensive terminology and currently used loss functions and their optimization techniques. 

\begin{definition}[Feature and Label Space] \label{def: feature and label space}
    Let $\mathcal{X}$ be the so-called \emph{feature space} and $\mathcal{Y}$ the \emph{label space}. Let further
    \begin{equation*}
        f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}
    \end{equation*}
    be the underlying \emph{true classification function},
    mapping each feature to a label. 
\end{definition}
The general goal of all machine learning problems is to closely approximate that classification function $f$.

\begin{definition}[Loss Function]
    Let $\Xcal$ be a feature space, $\mathcal{Y}$ a label space, and $f:\Xcal \to \mathcal{Y}$ the underlying true
    classification function.
    Given some arbitrary function $\hat f_\theta: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ and a sample set
    $\{x_i, f(x_i)\}_{i=1}^N \subset \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ for some $N \in \N$, we define
    the general loss function as
    \begin{equation*}
        \Lcal(\hat f_\theta \vert \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \{f(x_1), \dots, f(x_N)\}) \in \R^+,
    \end{equation*}
    such that for $f$ being the function from Definition (\ref{def: feature and label space})
    \begin{equation*}
        \Lcal(f \vert \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \{f(x_1), \dots, f(x_N)\}) = 0.
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Neural Network Weights] \label{def: neural network weights}
    Let $d, L, N_L \in \N$ and 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi = \left((A_1, b_1), \dots, (A_L, b_L)\right)
    \end{equation*}
    be some neural network with input dimension $d$, $L$ layers and output dimension $N_L$.
    Define the \emph{masks of $\Phi$} via
    \begin{equation*}
        I_l := \{ (i, j) \, : \, [A_l]_{ij} \neq 0 \}, \quad J_l := \{i \, : \, [b_l]_i \neq 0 \},
    \end{equation*}
    for each layer $1 \leq l \leq L$. We further set the \emph{admissible weight space} as
    \begin{equation*}
        \Theta(\Phi) := \prod_{l=1}^L \left( \R^{I_l} \times \R^{J_l} \right),
    \end{equation*}
    and its elements $\theta \in \Theta(\Phi)$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        \theta = \left( (\theta_{l, ij}^A)_{(i, j) \in I_l}, (\theta_{l, i}^b)_{i \in J_l} \right)_{l=1}^L.
    \end{equation*}
    Then $\Phi(\theta) = 
    ((\hat A_1(\theta), \hat b_1(\theta)), \dots, (\hat A_L(\theta), \hat b_L(\theta)))$
    can be uniquely defined for a choice of $\theta \in \Theta(\Phi)$ via the lifting
    \begin{equation*}
        \hat A_l(\theta)_{ij} = 
        \begin{cases}
            \theta^A_{l,ij}, & (i,j)\in I_l,\\
            0, & \text{otherwise},
        \end{cases}
        \qquad
        \hat b_l(\theta)_{i} =
        \begin{cases}
            \theta^b_{l,i}, & i\in J_l,\\
            0, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    This allows us to classify realizations of a neural network uniquely by
    a map, when knowing
    its architecture and a choice of neural network weights 
    $\theta \in \Theta(\Phi)$, via
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi(\theta)) : \R^d \to \R^{N_L}.
    \end{equation*}
    In particular, we know that if $\Phi$ has architecture $\Acal_\Phi$, then there is $e \in \Theta(\Phi)$,
    such that $\Phi(e) = \Acal_\Phi$.
\end{definition}

This definition will be used to address the training and associated \emph{freezing} of networks for online computation.
Namely, it provides the liberty to separate already trained, or frozen, neural networks from ones, that are still
being optimized.
Furthermore, it does not hinder any results, since all approximation theoretic results are posed in an 
"existence of architecture" context anyway.

The natural next question is, how such optimization might look. To briefly address this, we
provide the reader with a crude overview of the techniques, we will be using in our implementation.
The main goal here, is to minimize the associated loss function for some neural network $\Phi$ 
with regard to some true classification function $f$, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
    \underset{\theta \in \Theta(\Acal_\Phi)}{\operatorname{arg \, min}} \,\, 
    \Lcal\left(R_\rho(\Phi(\theta)) \, \vert \,  \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \{f(x_1), \dots, f(x_N)\}\right)
\end{equation*}

In the following, a brief introduction to the topics of batching, normalization, optimizers and
schedulars is given.

\paragraph{Batching}

The term \emph{batch} refers to a stack of a sample $\{x_i, f(x_i)\}_{i=1}^N \subset \Xcal \times \mathcal{Y}$.
This usually means, stacking along a new dimension. Conventionally this is the first dimension and the
batches are chosen as large as possible, but such that a clustering of features is avoided.

\paragraph{Normalization}

In computer science the specific order of numbers is important, 
since scaling can affect the problem. This can be mitigated, by 
a priori normalizing the input data and the output data. For example, consider a one-dimensional sample 
$\{x_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N \subset \R \times \R$.
Then the \emph{min-max normalization} for that sample is given via 
\begin{equation*}
    x_i \mapsto \frac{x_i - x_\text{min}}{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}} \in [0, 1], \quad y_i \mapsto 
    \frac{y_i - y_\text{min}}{y_\text{max} - y_\text{min}} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation*}
where we set up the maximum and minimum via
\begin{equation*}
    x_\text{max} = \max\limits_{i = 1, \dots, N} x_i, \quad x_\text{min} = \min\limits_{i = 1, \dots, N} x_i,
\end{equation*}
and for $y_\text{min}, y_\text{max}$ accordingly. It is important to remember to 
denormalize the solution for evaluation and comparative analysis with the deterministic inverse
operation. For our later example, we will switch this into
a multidimensional setting.

\paragraph{Optimizer}

The choice of an optimization method is crucial for the practical performance of neural networks. 
The original gradient descent scheme with stochastic approximation dates 
back to Robbins and Monro \cite{robbins1951}, 
and became widely adopted in the form of backpropagation \cite{rumelhart1986}. 
A first improvement was the introduction of momentum terms \cite{qian1999}, 
followed by adaptive step-size methods such as 
Adagrad \cite{duchi2011} and RMSProp \cite{tieleman2012}. 
The most popular adaptive optimizer in modern deep learning is Adam \cite{kingma2015}, 
which combines momentum with adaptive learning rates, and its decoupled variant AdamW 
for improved regularization \cite{loshchilov2019}. 
In our implementations we will mainly rely on AdamW.

\paragraph{Schedular}

Learning rate schedulers control the step-size of the optimizer during training. 
While constant learning rates are sufficient for convergence in theory, 
in practice carefully designed schedules can significantly improve both training stability 
and generalization. 
One well-established approach is the use of \emph{cosine annealing with warm restarts} (SGDR) 
introduced by Loshchilov and Hutter \cite{loshchilov2017}, 
which periodically reduces and resets the learning rate. 
Another popular technique is the class of cyclical learning rates \cite{smith2017}. 
In this work, we adopt cosine warm restarts due to their good empirical performance on PDE-related problems.
