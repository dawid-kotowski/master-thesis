\chapter{Formulation of the General Problem} \label{chapter: problem}

Let $\Theta \subset \R^{p}$ and $\Theta' \subset \R^q$ be compact and denote the geometric and physical parameter spaces 
respectively. 
Further we let $\Omega \subset \R^d$ be some Lipschitz domain and $[0, T]$ be a time interval
for some $T > 0$ of a parametric time-dependent PDE, 
i.e. for any $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\nu \in \Theta'$
\begin{equation*}
    u^{\mu, \nu} := u(\mu, \nu) , \quad \text{in a sufficiently smooth space},
\end{equation*}
is the (classical) solution of the PDE, for the choice of the space depending on the given
specified problem, if
\begin{equation*}\begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t} u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) + \mathcal{L}(\mu, \nu)u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) + 
    \mathcal{N}(u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t), \mu, \nu) 
    = f(x, t; \mu, \nu), \quad &(x, t) \in \Omega \times [0, T], \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \mathcal{B}(\mu, \nu) u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) = g_N(x, t; \mu, \nu) &(x, t) \in \partial \Omega \times [0, T], \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) = u_0^{\mu, \nu}(x) &(x, t) \in \Omega \times \{0 \}, \\
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where $\mathcal{L}$ represents a linear, and $\mathcal{N}$ represents a non-linear integro-differential operator.
Using calculus of variation or distribution theory, 
one can then formulate a weak form of the problem and find a general space, most commonly
Sobolev (for elliptic problems with coersive properties), Bochner (for parabolic problems with coersive properties
on the stationary part) or discontinuous Galerkin spaces (for hyperbolic problems with enough regularity), on which
a weak solution can be defined. Such a space $\Hcal$ is generally some Banach space, most often a subspace of
$L^2$.

This weak solution will have less regularity imposed than the strong formulation, 
but this sometimes allows us to retrieve existence and uniqueness results. 
Quite famously, there are counter examples of course; the Navier-Stokes Equation in three 
dimensions poses one such result, where a weak form exists and numerical methods have been developed, which
can provide a solution in special cases, but existence remains unproven in general. 
As mentioned beforehand, we will completely omit the exact transformation
of the strong PDE formulation into a weak one, and simple assume that this can be done:

Say we (can) discretize the problem in the spacial domain $\Omega$ with $N_h$ degrees of freedom, i.e. 
let $\Hcal_h \subset \Hcal$ be a Hilbert space with $\Hcal_h \cong \R^{N_h}$, a scalar product 
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\Hcal_h}$ and a corresponding norm $\norm{\cdot}_{\Hcal_h}$,
such that the resulting full order model solution can be identified using the formulation
\begin{equation} \label{eq: fom solution identity}
    t \mapsto u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j = 1}^{N_h} [\uh(t)]_j \, \varphi_j(\mu) \in C^1([0, T]; \Hcal_h) 
\end{equation}
with $\{\varphi_j(\mu)\}_{j=1}^{N_h}$ a basis of $\Hcal_h$ for each $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\uh(t) \in \R^{N_h}$ 
for each $t \in [0, T]$, and the underlying
problem for the coefficients
\begin{equation} \label{eq: fom ppde}
    \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    M(\mu) \frac{\partial}{\partial{t}} \uh(t) + A(\mu, \nu) \uh(t) + 
    N(\uh(t), \mu, \nu)= f(t; \mu, \nu)  \quad &t \in [0, T], \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \uh(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t=0, \end{cases} 
\end{equation}
where we assume 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[$\bullet$] $M: \Theta \to \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ to send the geometrical parameter to a mass matrix,
    \item[$\bullet$] $A: \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ to send the set to a symmetric positive definite stiffness matrix,
    \item[$\bullet$] $N: \R^{N_h} \times \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h}$ to be some non-linear term,
    \item[$\bullet$] $f: [0, T] \times \R^{N_h} \times \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h}$ 
    to be the corresponding source term of the equation.
\end{enumerate}    
Additionally let $u_0: \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h}$ be the inital data. 
The mass matrix is defined for each $\mu \in \Theta$ as follows
\begin{equation*}
    M(\mu) = \begin{bmatrix}
        \langle \varphi_1(\mu), \varphi_1(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h} & \cdots & \langle \varphi_1(\mu), \varphi_{N_h}(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h} \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        \langle \varphi_{N_h}(\mu), \varphi_1(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h} & \cdots & \langle \varphi_{N_h}(\mu), \varphi_{N_h}(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h}
        \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
This allows us to equip $\R^{N_h}$, the space of the coefficients of any solution in $\Hcal_h$, with the following
norm
\begin{equation} \label{eq: definition mass matrix norm}
    \norm{\uht} := \sqrt{(\uht)^T \, M(\mu) \, \uht},
\end{equation}
which is, in fact, the equivalent of the norm above, i.e. $\norm{\uht} = \norm{u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu)}_{\Hcal_h}$.

\notdone{Introducing the mass matrix norm will make all data driven procedures somewhat intrusive, since the norm will
depend on mu and nu, maybe not even a problem, since the choice needs to be done a priori, but what about pod and projections}

Note that it is indeed possible to arrive at that discretization without skipping to a weak formulation, e.g.
using finite differences or finite volume schemes, but since any existence statement needs a more general space
than $C^n$ to guarantee completeness and hence imposes some identity using a basis, 
we need only to infer such a procedure. For the interested reader, an explicit discretization using finite volume
schemes can be seen in \cite{haasdonk2008pod}.

This formulation establishes smoothness with regard to time, 
however we will implicitly further discretize in time using
$\{t_k\}_{k=1}^{N_t} := \Tcal$ time steps by gathering data only at these points. This is called
\emph{method of lines approach} \cite[p. 278]{hesthaven2022rb}. 


\begin{definition}[Solution Data] \label{def: solution data}    
    Following this, we may denote with
    \begin{equation*}
        \uht := \uh(t) \in \R^{N_h}, \quad \text{for }\mu \in \Theta, \nu \in \Theta' \text{ and all } t \in \Tcal,
    \end{equation*}
    a finite dimensional \emph{solution snapshot} and
    \begin{equation*}
        \uh := [\uh(t)]_{t \in \Tcal} \in \R^{N_h \times N_t}, 
        \quad \text{for }\mu \in \Theta \text{ and } \nu \in \Theta',
    \end{equation*}
    with $N_t := \vert \Tcal \vert$ a finite dimensional \emph{solution trajectory} of the discretized parametric PDE.
    Furthermore, let $\Pcal_1 \subset \Theta$, $\Pcal_2 \subset \Theta'$ and $\Pcal = \Pcal_1 \times
    \Pcal_2$ be some finite parameter data with respective sizes $N_{s_1}, N_{s_2} \in \N \cup \{\infty\}$ and $N_s = N_{s_1} N_{s_2}$.
\end{definition}


Mainly, we stated the problem framework, 
to know with which class of
of PDEs we will be working with. Hence any regularity assumption is only given here in order to assert an outlook on the
use of $L^p$ norms for the error between the weak solution $u \in \Hcal$, the finite dimenisonal solution $u_h \in \Hcal_h$ 
and the approximation of that solution $\hat{u}_h \in \Hcal_h$ (of reduced order) using ROM methods.

It is the latter ones, we will be concerned with in this thesis, 
which are however clearly embedded in $L^2$, since they are finite dimensional and bounded by an upcoming assumption.

From this point forward, we want to consistenly rely on the following few assumptions, which regulate the 
discrete problem above. The first Assumption (\ref{assumption KnW decay}) justifies a nuanced approach regarding the
geometric parameter $\mu$ and the physical parameter $\nu$. The second Assumption (\ref{assumption sample}) is necessary
for the derivation of any convergence result to a true parameter-to-solution map, 
when sampling an ever growing number of examples, while the third Assumption (\ref{assumption parameter-to-solution map})
imposes regularity on the mentioned parameter-to-solution map. Interestingly enough, it is that Assumption, which ensures
the existence of a true solution of the discrete problem for any specified parameter choice. Observing of course, that
this does not translate into the solvability for the upstream general problem.
The last Assumption (\ref{assumption perfect embedding}) gives us the ability to later use an Autoencoder for a reduction.


\begin{assumption} \label{assumption KnW decay}
    Let $\mathcal{S} := \{ \uht \in \R^{N_h} \, \vert \, (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times [0, T] \}$ 
    denote the \emph{total solution manifold} and $\mathcal{S}_{\mu, t} := 
    \{ \uht \in \R^{N_h} \, \vert \, \nu \in \Theta' \}$ denote the \emph{solution 
    submanifold} for a fixed set $(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T]$, then we assume that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item the linear KnW of $\mathcal{S}$ decays slowly, i.e. 
        \begin{equation*}
            d_N(\mathcal{S}) \leq C N^{-\alpha},
        \end{equation*}
        where $0 < \alpha \leq 1$ and $C > 0$ some constant.
        \item the linear KnW of $\mathcal{S}_{\mu, t}$ decays quickly for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $t \in [0, T]$, i.e. 
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_{(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T]} d_N(\mathcal{S}_{\mu, t}) \leq C' N^{-\beta},    
        \end{equation*}
        where $\beta > 1$ and $C' > 0$ some constant.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

This is mostly satisfied for problems in which the transport dominated or non-linear part is chained
to the parameter $\mu$, and
any coersive or elliptic operator is most heavily influence by $\nu$.

\begin{assumption} \label{assumption sample}    
    Let $\Theta, \Theta'$ and $[0, T]$
    for $T > 0$ as above. We assume that all $N_s \in \N_{\geq 2}$ parameter data points according to 
    Definition (\ref{def: solution data}) are sampled uniformly and 
    iid in the joint parameter space $\Theta \times \Theta'$, 
    while a uniform grid is employed for the time variable, 
    $\Tcal = \{\Delta t, 2 \Delta t, \dots, N_t \Delta t \}$, 
    where $N_t \in \mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}$, $N_{s_1}$ and $N_{s_2}$ 
    are the respective number of random samples in the parameter spaces $\Theta$ and $\Theta'$,
    $N_s = N_{s_1} N_{s_2} \in \mathbb{N}$ is the total sample size, and $\Delta t = T / N_t$.
\end{assumption}

We remark, that the equidistant spacing of the time samples is a matter of convenience, 
and could be loosened under some constraints.

\begin{assumption} \label{assumption parameter-to-solution map}
    Let $\mathcal{G}: \Theta \times \Theta' \times [0, T] \to \R^{N_h}$ be the parameter-to-solution map, 
    mapping $(\mu, \nu, t) \mapsto \uht$. Here we assume that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $m = 
        \underset{(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times \mathcal{T}}{\operatorname{ess}\, \inf}
        \norm{u_h(\mu, \nu, t)} > 0, \, M = 
        \underset{(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times \mathcal{T}}{\operatorname{ess}\, \sup}
        \norm{u_h(\mu, \nu, t)} < \infty$,
        \item $\mathcal{G}$ is Lipschitz-continuous with the constant $L > 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

A very important fact follows already directly from the presumably allowed well-posed definition of such a map; the solution 
$u_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$ exists and is unique, in at least an $L^2$-embedded sense, since the time-derivate can
be relaxed using a variational argument. 

\begin{assumption} \label{assumption perfect embedding}
    Let $N \in \N$ be fixed. We assume that for $s, s'$ sufficiently large, there are two maps 
    $\psi_*: \R^n \to \R^N$ and $\psi_*': \R^N \to \R^n$ 
    which attain the perfect nonlinear embedding of the reduced solution manifold, 
    as given in Definition (\ref{def: nonlinear kolmogorov}) for any $n \leq 2(p + q) + 3$, i.e.
    for $\mathcal{S}_N := \{ q(\mu, \nu, t) = \A^T u(\mu, \nu, t) \, \vert \, (\mu, \nu, t) 
    \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times \Tcal \}$, where $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ is the POD matrix,
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta_n(\mathcal{S}_N) = 0.
    \end{equation*}
\end{assumption}



Every following Reduced Order Model will pursue the universal (and vague) following goal.

\begin{goal}
    Find
    \begin{equation*}
        \hat{\mathcal{G}}: \Theta \times \Theta' \times [0, T] \to \R^{N_h}; \quad (\mu, \nu, t) \mapsto \uhthat,
    \end{equation*}
    such that for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\nu \in \Theta'$
    \begin{equation*}
        \sup\limits_{t \in [0, 1]}\norm{\uht - \uhthat} = 
        \sup\limits_{t \in [0, 1]}\norm{u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) - \hat{u}_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu)}_{\Hcal_h}
    \end{equation*}
    is small.
\end{goal}

In general, we remind ourselves of the starting situation: $\Hcal_h \subset \Hcal$ is a finite dimensional
approximation space of our weak problem. As mentioned before, we also have employed discretized time-steps.
We therefore have something along the lines of
\begin{equation} \label{eq: full discrete ppde}
    \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    M(\mu) \hat{\partial}_t \uh(t) + A(\mu, \nu) \uh(t) + 
    N(\uh(t), \mu, \nu)= f(t; \mu, \nu)  \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \uh(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t=0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
for $\hat{\partial}_t$ being a discrete approximation of the time-derivative and $\uh(t) \in \R^{N_h}$ for
$t \in [t_k, t_{k+1})$ and some $k = 0, \dots, N_t - 1$ the piecewise constant coefficient vector for the true solution
\begin{equation*}
    u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j = 1}^{N_h} [\uh(t)]_j \, \varphi_j(\mu) \in \Hcal_h, 
    \text{ for each } t \in  [t_k, t_{k+1}) \text{ and } (\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'. 
\end{equation*}



\section{Intrusive Reduced Order Models}


In this section we want to introduce some such \emph{intrusive} methods for the dimensionality reduction of the finite
dimensional parametric PDE. The name "intrusive" means to symbolize the "intrusion" of the method into the formulation
of the PDE and the specifications of its operators. Coinciding with that, there are methods for linear and non-linear
problems respectively, as the more simple and light weight reduction techniques can leverage an abstract linear variational
problem to achieve a (Petrov-)Galerkin projection, whereas any non-linear problem, so a problem with $N \neq 0$
according to Equation (\ref{eq: fom ppde}), utilize the specifications of the non-linear operator to achieve reduction.

\begin{remark}[Historic Literature]
    The development of intrusive reduced order models (ROMs) dates back to the 
    late 1970s and 1980s, when methods for projection onto low-dimensional trial 
    spaces were systematically introduced in the context of parametrized PDEs. 
    Early formulations of the \emph{reduced basis (RB) method} can be found in the 
    control and approximation theory literature, e.g.\ in the pioneering works of 
    Courant and Hilbert on variational methods~\cite{couranthilbert1953}, and in 
    the first computational implementations by Noor and coworkers 
    in structural mechanics~\cite{noor1980}. These ideas were later rigorously 
    developed into the modern RB methodology by Patera in the mid-1980s 
    \cite{patera1984}, and subsequently advanced by Maday, Rovas, Prud'homme and 
    others in the 1990s and 2000s~\cite{maday2002,prudhomme2002}.  

    In parallel, the POD emerged as a 
    computational tool for obtaining optimal low-rank approximations of 
    dynamical systems, building on statistical formulations dating back to 
    Karhunen and Loève. The introduction of POD-Galerkin for intrusive model 
    reduction was consolidated in applied mechanics and fluid dynamics in the 
    1990s and early 2000s (see~\cite{holmes1996,kunischvolkwein2001}).  

    As nonlinear problems became central, the cost of evaluating nonlinear terms 
    prompted the development of \emph{hyper-reduction techniques}, such as the 
    Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM)~\cite{barrault2004} and its discrete 
    variants (DEIM)~\cite{chaturantabut2010}, which remain core components of 
    modern intrusive ROM frameworks.  
\end{remark}


We will start with the framework of projection-based methods, that necessitate a problem with $N = 0$.

All of the following methods will be discussed broadly, as the thesis concerns itself mostly
with the later data-driven ROMs. 



\begin{definition}[Reduced Basis]
    Let $\Hcal_n \subset \operatorname{span}(u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) \in \Hcal_h \, \vert \,
    (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal)$ be an $n$-dimensional subspace
    of $\Hcal_h$, then if $\{\hat{\varphi}_j\}_{j=1}^n$ is an orthonormalized basis of $\Hcal_n$, we call this the
    \emph{reduced basis}.
\end{definition}

From this starting point, the goal of all \emph{reduced basis methods} is to find a reduced basis $\{\hat{\varphi}_j(\mu)\}_{j=1}^n$ 
and $\uhhat(t) \in \R^n$ for $n \ll N_h$, such that
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{u}_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} [\uhhat(t)]_j \, \hat{\varphi}_j(\mu) \in \Hcal_n \subset \Hcal_h,
\end{equation*}
where, in a yet vague formulation,
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{u}_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) \approx u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) \quad \text{for all } \mu \in \Theta, \nu \in \Theta'
    \text{ and } t \in [0, T].
\end{equation*}
In the terminology the RB methods are a setup for many different ROMs, but most often appear for so-called
projection-based ROMs, which itself are 
intrusive ROMs. They help with the identification of a suitable basis to project upon.
Two prominent examples of such reduced basis methods in the context of projection-based ROMs can be found in the following. 
Both will enhibit intrinsically an \emph{offline}
and an \emph{online} part in the algorithm, whose separation we will mostly neglect. However, when remarking something
about the offline procedure, we usually talk about the part of the algorithm that can be collected a priori to 
its purposed deployment. Here this is the collection and reduction of dimension using a reduced basis via samples. 
On the other hand, online refers to its counterpart; the many-query element of the procedure, such as the evaluation of the 
best-guess parameter-to-solution map. This terminology is used often throughout the literature as it instinctively 
makes sense in a software development environment.


\subsection{Galerkin Proper Orthogonal Decomposition}

As a prerequisit for Galerkin orthogonality, we assume here that the weak form giving rise to the equation at hand derives
from an abstract bilinear form in the stationary part. This is justified if the non-linear summand is zero. The source
for this subsection is \cite[p. 137]{quarteroni2015rb}.

The POD as introduced in Definition (\ref{def: POD}) is one of the most common methods for a reduction in
a classical sense. We can use the system above to solve for the solution using a Galerkin projection for the following
Ansatz:
\begin{equation} \label{eq: identity for pod galerkin}
    \hat{u}_h(x, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j=1}^n [\uhhat(t)]_j \, \xi_j(x),
\end{equation}
where $x \in \Omega$, $t \in [0, 1]$, $(\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'$ and 
$\xi_j$ for $1 \leq j \leq n$ are the POD modes, as given by the construction of the POD in Defintition 
(\ref{def: POD}) with $\G := \I_{N_h}$, and $\uhhat(t) \in \R^n$
is the coefficient vector, that solves the following reduced equation
\begin{equation} \label{eq: reduced full discrete ppde}
    \begin{cases}
        \A^T M(\mu) \A \hat{\partial}_t \uhhat(t) + \A^T A(\mu, \nu) \A \uhhat(t)
        = \A^T f(t; \mu, \nu) \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\
        \uhhat(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t = 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Note, that in this construction $\A$ is \emph{not} $M(\mu)$-orthogonal, thus losing certain projection qualities
with regard to the inherent $L^2$-norm of the underlying Hilbert space $\Hcal_h$.
The parameter-to-solution map can be recovered by the following Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod-galerkin}).

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{POD-Galerkin} \label{algo: pod-galerkin}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \State \textbf{Input:} $\left(\mu_j, \nu_j\right)_{j=1}^{N_s}$ data sample
        and $n < N_h$ reduced dimension
        \State \textbf{Output:} approximated parameter-to-solution-map $(\mu, \nu) \mapsto 
        \hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$
        \State Compute $\{u_h^{\mu_j, \nu_j, t_{k_m}}\}_{m=1}^{N_t}$ from 
        Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}) (with $N = 0$)
        \State $(\xi_j, \sigma_j)_{j=1}^n \gets \text{POD}(u_h^{\mu_j,\nu_j,t_{k_m}}, n)$
        \State $\A \gets \text{stack}(\xi_j, j=1, \dots, n)$
        \State Build reduced Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde}) 
        \State For each $(\mu, \nu)$ solve Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde})
        \State Recover $\hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$ via Equation (\ref{eq: identity for pod galerkin})
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}





\subsection{Greedy Proper Orthogonal Decomposition}





An introduction to this method in the stationary and well-posed case can be found in \cite{siena2022pod}. As done
previously in the subsection above, we assume $N = 0$ in Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}). This gives us a 
coersive abstract bilinear form for the stationary part. Additionally let $\eta : \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R$ be 
a function estimating the error of projection under a specific parameter set, i.e. such that 
\begin{equation*}
    \sup\limits_{t \in \Tcal} \, \norm{u_h(\mu, \nu, t) - \hat{u}_h(\mu, \nu, t)} \leq \eta(\mu, \nu),
\end{equation*}

for $(\mu, \nu) \in \Pcal$. Purely using POD-Galerkin can encounter major pitfalls, namely if the range of $\Pcal$ is extensive, 
since then calculating a singluar value decomposition for the correlation matrix might get too cumbersome to provide all
areas of interest in the set. Moreover, to obtain the snapshots for fixed parameters, a complete time-trajectory must
be computed, which entails a lot of overhead of otherwise useless information. To overcome this \cite{grepl2005rb} proposed,
and later \cite{haasdonk2008pod} improved upon, the mixed POD-Greedy approach, which takes a POD over the time-trajectories
(or, in the improved version, the error trajectories) while using the Greedy approach to handle the parameter samples.
It follows a similar workflow, as the POD-Galerkin, since the only thing, that changes is the choice of basis, and the fact that the size
of the basis is chosen according to the tolerance in the offline part of the algorithm, i.e.
\begin{equation} \label{eq: identity for pod greedy galerkin}
    \hat{u}_h(x, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j=1}^N [\uhhat(t)]_j \, \xi_j(x),
\end{equation}
with $x \in \Omega$, $t \in [0, 1]$, $(\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'$ 
identifies the reduced solution of dimension $N$, where $\xi_j$ are the basis functions obtained from the offline procedure
in Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod-greedy-galerkin}) for $1 \leq j \leq N$ and the
coefficient vector being the solution to 
\begin{equation} \label{eq: reduced full discrete ppde greedy}
    \begin{cases}
        \V_\text{rb}^T M(\mu) \V_\text{rb} \hat{\partial}_t \uhhat(t) + \V_\text{rb}^T A(\mu, \nu) \V_\text{rb} \uhhat(t)
        = \V_\text{rb}^T f(t; \mu, \nu) \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\
        \uhhat(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t = 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Note again, that $\V_\text{rb}$ is \emph{not} $M(\mu)$-orthonormal.
The complexity of the offline computation for this Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod-greedy-galerkin}) is additive, and not multplicative, in the number of 
training data $\Pcal$, which is a huge advantage when compared to the direct POD-Galerkin approach. 

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{POD-Greedy-Galerkin} \label{algo: pod-greedy-galerkin}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \State \textbf{Input: } \texttt{tol}, $\mathcal{Z} = 0$, $N = 0$, $(\mu_j, \nu_j)_{j=1}^{N_s}$, $N_1$ and $N_2$
        \State \textbf{Output: } approximated parameter-to-solution-map $(\mu, \nu) \mapsto 
        \hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$
        \For{$j = 1,\dots,N_s$}
            \State Compute $\{u_h^{\mu_j, \nu_j, t_{k_m}}\}_{m=1}^{N_t}$ from 
            Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}) (with $N = 0$)
            \State $(\zeta_j, \lambda_j)_{j=1}^{N_1} \gets 
            \text{POD}(\{u_h^{\mu_j,\nu_j,t_{k_m}}\}_{m=1}^{N_t}, N_1)$
            \State $\mathcal{Z} \gets \{\mathcal{Z}, \{\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_{N_1}\}\}$
            \State $N \gets N + N_2$ 
            \State Compress: $(\xi_j, \sigma_j)_{j=1}^n \gets \text{POD}(\mathcal{Z}, N)$
            \State $(\mu_{j+1}, \nu_{j+1}) \gets \underset{(\mu, \nu) \in \Pcal}{\operatorname{arg\,max}} \, \eta(\mu, \nu)$
            \If{$\eta(\mu_{j+1}, \nu_{j+1}) > \texttt{tol}$}
                \State $j \gets j + 1$
            \Else

\section{Data-driven Reduced Order Models} \label{section: data-driven ROMs}




In contrast to the introduced intrusive ROMs above, the \emph{data-driven} alternatives aim to circumvent 
the direct use of the governing equations, and, as such, allow for more leniency toward unknown operator qualities.

\begin{remark}[Historic Literature]
    The origins of data-driven ROMs can be traced back to techniques in fluid 
    dynamics and control, where the objective was to extract coherent structures 
    or dominant modes from experimental measurements without explicit reliance on 
    the full Navier--Stokes equations. A first influential step was the development 
    of the POD for experimental data, 
    introduced in the turbulence community by Lumley~\cite{lumley1967}, and further 
    popularized in the coherent structure analysis of Sirovich~\cite{sirovich1987}. 
    While POD itself does not provide a dynamical law for the online phase, it laid the foundation for 
    representing complex systems with a small number of empirical modes.

    A decisive breakthrough came with the advent of the 
    \emph{Dynamic Mode Decomposition} (DMD)~\cite{schmid2010}, which may be 
    interpreted as a data-driven approximation of the infinite-dimensional Koopman 
    operator~\cite{rowley2009}. DMD represents temporal evolution as a best-fit 
    linear operator acting on snapshot data, thereby yielding both modal 
    decompositions and a surrogate model for prediction. This was one of the first 
    systematic attempts to construct a fully non-intrusive ROM.

    In parallel, regression-based approaches have been developed to learn reduced 
    operators directly from data, often under the name of 
    \emph{operator inference}~\cite{peherstorfer2016}, or in the form of 
    interpolation-based reduced models, e.g.\ POD with interpolation (PODI) 
    as in Barrault et al.~\cite{barrault2004}. These methods demonstrate that the 
    reduced dynamics can be inferred without Galerkin projection, either by 
    statistical regression or by interpolation in parameter space.
\end{remark}

For this section we intentionally do not give an explicit training algorithm for any of the methods presented,
since it can be infered with an optimizer of the readers choice from the discussion 
in Subsection (\ref{subsec: statistical learning}). With readability in mind, we thus only state the architecture
of the network at hand and its loss function. Most importantly, we assume that some choice of basis
for $\Hcal_h$ is already done a priori to the training of the network and independent of $\mu \in \Theta$
thus setting $\G := M(\mu)$ for all $\mu \in \Theta$ as our mass matrix. Under Equation
(\ref{eq: definition mass matrix norm}) this also implies a $\mu$-independent norm $\norm{\cdot}$, which in fact
allow us to use the POD matrix, if constructed with $\G$, as a true projector onto the span of $\A$ in an $L^2$ sense.

For clarity, we formulate the following as an assumption, since from experience, the reduction system across comparable
architectures can get confusing, and the reader is therefore encouraged to revisit this assumption, whenever
the general setting appears to be unclear.

\begin{assumption}[Dimension Hierarchy] \label{assumption: dimension hierarchy}
    Let for all following data-driven Reduced Order Models, the following natural number hierarchy be fixed
    \begin{equation*}
        n < N' < N \ll N_A \ll N_h.
    \end{equation*}
    Note, that "$\ll$" is an unspecified relation, meaning to symbolize a "large" difference.
\end{assumption}

A general Deep Learning based ROM (DL-ROM) first appeared in architecture and 
training in the paper \cite{fresca2020}; to properly approximate the potentially 
non-linear solution manifold of the high fidelity FOM, we employ an Autoencoder (AE), 
and on the reduced manifold we employ some other Deep Learning architecture, 
like the proposed Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network (DFNN), to learn the latent dynamics of the parametric PDE.
This concludes in the following approximation of the FOM coefficient solution $\uht \in \R^{N_h}$ for each 
$\mu \in \Theta$, $\nu \in \Theta'$ and $t \in \Tcal$
\begin{equation} \label{eq: dl rom solution}
    \uhthat = \left(R_\rho(\Psi_h(\theta^*_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta^*_\text{DF}))\right) (t; \mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h},
\end{equation}
where we set $\Psi_h = f^\text{D}_h$ for the decoder architecture with input dimension $n$ and output dimension $N_h$
(and $\Psi'_n = f^\text{E}_n$ the corresponding encoder architecture) of a convolutional AE. 
Then $\Psi'_n$ denotes the projection onto the reduced trial solution manifold and we 
let $\Phi_n = \Phi_n^\text{DF}$  
to be the DFNN architecture of input dimension $p + q + 1$ and output dimension $n$, 
mapping the dynamics of the parametric PDE onto the reduced trial solution manifold.

The subsequent training is then performed via a loss function of a convex combination of both, 
the projection error using the encoder and the dynamics error considering both the decoder and 
the feed forward network, i.e. for $\theta = (\theta_D, \theta_E, \theta_{DF})$ define the optimal weights via
the problem in
Definition (\ref{def: neural network weights}) with the loss function
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss function dl rom}
    \Lcal_\text{DL-ROM}\left(R_\rho(\Psi_h \odot \Phi_n(\theta)) \vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_h^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) =  \\
        \sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
        \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \frac{\omega_h}{2} \, 
        \norm{u_h^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
        \left(R_\rho(\Psi_h(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \\
        + \, \frac{1 - \omega_h}{2} \, \euknorm{R_\rho(\Psi'_n(\theta_\text{E}))(u_h^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}) 
        - R_\rho(\Phi_n^\text{DF}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2.
\end{multline}





\subsection{Proper Orthogonal Decomposition Deep Learning ROM} \label{subsec: pod-dl-rom}




The main idea of the POD-DL-ROM is the additional employment of an outer a priori reduction of DL-ROM problem using 
a POD. It can be found in \cite{fresca2022}. Note, that we
sway a bit from the source, since we are trying to achieve 
an estimate, which is close to the high-fidenlity solution
in an $L^2$-sense and not a euklidian one by applying the
mass matrix from the start of the section.
Such a reduction yields great benefits, such as the scalability in $N_h$ 
and reduction in computational cost for
the offline training. It is done by employing a POD (see Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod}))
on the stacked solution trajectories 
\begin{equation*}
    U := \left[u_h^{\mu_1, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert 
    u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_{N_{s_2}}}\right]^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}},
\end{equation*}
with the solution trajectories of Definition (\ref{def: solution data}), resulting in the POD matrix 
$\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ for the $N < N_h$ from Assumption (\ref{assumption: dimension hierarchy}). Then we can
define the pre-reduced solution snapshots by computing
\begin{equation*}
    u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \A^T \G \uht \in \R^{N} \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
By $\G$-orthonormality of $\A$, we can thus define the FOM approximate coefficient solution $\uhthat \in \R^{N_h}$ for each 
$\mu \in \Theta$, $\nu \in \Theta'$ and $t \in \Tcal$
\begin{equation} \label{eq: pod-dl-rom solution}
    \uhthat = \A \cdot \left(R_\rho(\Psi_A(\theta^*_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta^*_\text{DF}))\right) (t; \mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h},
\end{equation}
where we set $\Psi_N = f^\text{D}_{N}$ for the decoder architecture with input dimension $n$ and output dimension $N$
(and $\Psi'_n = f^\text{E}_n$ the corresponding encoder architecture) of a convolutional AE. 
Then $f^\text{E}_n$ denotes the projection onto the reduced trial solution manifold and we 
let $\Phi_n = \Phi_n^\text{DF}$  
to be the DFNN architecture of input dimension $p + q + 1$ and output dimension $n$ same as before.

The loss function has now the attribute of being fed only at most $N$ dimensional training data, and only
evaluating at most $N$ dimensional euklidian norms, hence greatly 
increasing performance, i.e. with $P$ being the network parallelization operator
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss function pod dl rom}
    \Lcal_\text{POD-DL-ROM}\left(R_\rho(P(\Psi_N \odot \Phi_n, \Psi'_n)(\theta)) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_\text{red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) =  \\
        \frac{1}{N_\text{data}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
        \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \bigg( \frac{\omega_h}{2} \, 
        \euknorm{u_\text{red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
        \left(R_\rho(\Psi_N(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \\
        + \, \frac{1 - \omega_h}{2} \, \euknorm{R_\rho(\Psi'_n(\theta_\text{E}))(u_\text{red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}) 
        - R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \bigg).
\end{multline}

\subsection{Deep Orthogonal Decomposition based ROMs}





A big advantage of the POD-DL-ROM is the fact, that under the assumption of a static basis 
$\{\xi_j\}_{j=1}^{N_h} \subset \Hcal_h$ of $\Hcal_h$ and its associated mass matrix $\G$ and $\Hcal_h$ equivalent norm
$\norm{\cdot}$, we may reduce the high dimensional loss function in its essence to a managable computational complexity.
However, this so far ignored the particular problem in sight; it ignores Assumption (\ref{assumption KnW decay}),
which guarantees a significant reduction cost, when employing a $(\mu, t)$-independent projector.
To circumvent this Kolmogorov $N$-width barrier for $N \ll N_h$, one can instead of using a POD for the choice of reduced basis,
invoke a Neural Network based linear projector, that fits a basis superposition dependend on $\mu$ and $t$,
and can thus exhibit a further reduction to $N' < N$ under a similar value for the worst-case projection,
i.e. the Kolmogorov $N'$-width.

The following technically depicts a RB method. 
\begin{definition}[Deep Orthogonal Decomposition] \label{def: DOD}
    Recollect the dimensional hierarchy of Assumption (\ref{assumption: dimension hierarchy}).
    Employ a POD (according to Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod})) with $\G$
    given as above using
    the stacked solution trajectories
    \begin{equation*}
        U := \left[u_h^{\mu_1, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert 
        u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_{N_{s_2}}}\right]^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}},
    \end{equation*}
    with the solution trajectories of Definition (\ref{def: solution data}), resulting in the POD matrix 
    $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$.
    Now set up for the inner module of the Deep Orthogonal Decomposition a Neural Network architecture 
    $\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}$ with input dimension $p + 1$ and output dimension $N_A \times N'$
    such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi_{\tilde{\V}} = \text{ORTH} \circ \text{STACK} \circ (P((R_1 \odot s), \dots, (R_N \odot s)))(\mu, t),
    \end{equation*}
    where $P$ is denoting the network parallelization operator as given in Definition (\ref{def: network parallelization})
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $s$ is a Deep Feed Forward Neural Network architecture with input dimension $p + 1$ and output dimension $l$,
        \item $R_1, \dots, R_N$ are a collection of Deep Feed Forward Neural Network architectures
        with input dimensions $l$ and output dimensions $N_A$,
        \item $\text{STACK}: \R^{N_A \cdot N'} \to \R^{N_A \times N'}$ is a canonical stacking operator, 
        \item $\text{ORTH}: \R^{N_A \times N'} \to \R^{N_A \times N'}$ is an orthonormalization unit, that orthonormalizes
        any given matrix $W \in \R^{N_A \times N'}$ into $\tilde{W} \in \R^{N_A \times N'}$ such that 
        $\text{span}(W) = \text{span}(\tilde W)$. This is most often done using a QR decomposition \cite{golubvanloan2013}. 
    \end{enumerate}
    The realization of the network $\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}$ for some $\mu \in \Theta$, $t \in [0, T]$ and
    any chosen neural network weight $\theta_\text{DOD} \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ 
    according to Definition (\ref{def: neural network weights}) will be abbreviated with
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: inner module DOD}
        \tilde{\V}_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) := R_\rho(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}(\theta_\text{DOD}))(\mu, t).
    \end{equation}
    For any choice of weights, this is called the \emph{inner module of the Deep Orthogonal Decomposition}, or in short 
    the inner DOD module.

    \emph{The Deep Orthogonal Decomposition} can hence be achieved by upward projection under the POD matrix $\A$ of 
    the inner DOD module, i.e. a realization of the underlying DOD neural network for a choice of weights
    $\theta_\text{DOD} \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ is attained for some
    $\mu \in \Theta$ and $t \in [0, T]$ by
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: DOD}
        \V_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) := \A \cdot \tilde{\V}_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) \in \R^{N_h \times N'}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}


Nonetheless this does not warrant a "good" DOD, just a defined one. 
For this one needs to assert a qualitative loss function and some data, which we will collect by pre-reduction
in the same manner as before, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
    u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \A^T \G \uht \in \R^{N_A} \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
Then the loss function with regard to that data can be defined as follows
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss dod}
    \Lcal_\text{DOD}\left(R_\rho(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}(\theta_\text{DOD})) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,k}, 
    \{u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) \\
    = \frac{1}{N_\text{data}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
    \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \euknorm{u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
    \tilde{\V}_{\mu_i, k \Delta t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) \tilde{\V}^T_{\mu_i, k \Delta t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) 
    u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}}^2.
\end{multline}
Note, that this is indeed again a $N_A$-dimensional loss function, thanks to the $\G$-orthonormality
of the POD matrix.
In some abuse of notation, we will sometimes refer to the optimized version of the DOD network as the DOD.

Notice, that we have not yet performed any actual search for the approximate parameter-to-solution map, that maps
a specific instance of parameters to a coefficient time-trajectory, that can then be used to build the
solution map in the underlying high fidelity Hilber space approximating the FOM solution in 
Equation (\ref{eq: fom solution identity}).

In this paper, we propose two novel data-driven ROMs, based on the DOD, to find an approximate solution-to-parameter 
map. These are the \emph{Deep Orthogonal Decomposition with Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network} (DOD+DFNN) and the
\emph{Deep Orthogonal Decomposition based Deep Learning ROM} (DOD-DL-ROM). The former indeed symbolizes a general 
approach hence including the latter, but, with some abuse of notation, we admit the DOD+DFNN a naive direct
Deep Feed Forward architecture for the determination of the coefficient, while the DOD-DL-ROM is an adaptation
of the DL-ROM architecture introduced in the header of the section.

\subsubsection{DOD+DFNN} \label{subsec: DOD+DFNN}

Let $\Phi_{N'} := \Phi_{N'}^\text{DF}$ 
be some Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network architecture
with input dimension $p+q+1$ and output dimension $N'$, then the approximate parameter-to-solution map for
a fixed weight $\theta = (\theta_\text{DOD}, \theta_\text{DF}) \in 
\Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}) \times \Theta(\Phi_n^\text{DF})  $ according to 
Definition (\ref{def: neural network weights}) can be given for each $(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta'
\times [0, T]$ via
\begin{equation} \label{eq: dod+dnn solution}
    \uhthat = \V_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) \cdot R_\rho(\Phi_{N'}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu, \nu, t) \in \R^{N_h}.
\end{equation}

Subsequently the training is done in series and not in parallel, as the optimal DOD parameter $\theta^*_\text{DOD}$
needs to be found first. Secondly, the $\G$-orthonormality of the DOD can be leveraged with
\begin{equation*}
    u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \V_{\mu, t}(\theta^*_\text{DOD})^T \G \uht \in \R^{N'} 
    \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal,
\end{equation*}
to further reduce the training data and finally define the loss function for the DOD+DFNN architecture
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss dod+dnn}
    \Lcal_\text{DOD+DNN}\left(R_\rho(\Phi_{N'}(\theta_\text{DF})) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,k}, 
    \{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) \\
    = \frac{1}{N_\text{data}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
    \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \euknorm{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
    R_\rho(\Phi_{N'}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2.
\end{multline}

This is a rather obvious extension of \cite{franco2024}, but works surprisingly well. 
It will be the basic benchmark to overcome for the following algorithms. 





\subsubsection{DOD-DL-ROM} \label{subsec: dod-dl-rom}





Let $\Psi_{N'} := f_{N'}^\text{D}$ be a decoder architecture with input dimension $n$ and output dimension $N'$ 
(and $\Psi'_n := f_n^\text{E}$ the corresponding encoder architecture), and $\Phi_n := \Phi_n^\text{DF}$
be some Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network architecture with input dimension $p+q+1$ and output dimension $n$
then the approximate parameter-to-solution map for
a fixed weight $\theta = (\theta_\text{DOD}, \theta_\text{D}, \theta_\text{DF}) \in 
\Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}) \times \Theta(f_N^\text{D}) \times \Theta(\Phi_n^\text{DF})$ according to 
Definition (\ref{def: neural network weights}) can be given for each $(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta'
\times [0, T]$ via
\begin{equation} \label{eq: dod-dl-rom solution}
    \uhthat = \V_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) \cdot 
    \left(R_\rho(\Psi_{N'}(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (t; \mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h}
\end{equation}

Same, as for the DOD+DNN, the training is done in series. One observes, that the training data can be again
reduced with
\begin{equation*}
    u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \V_{\mu, t}(\theta^*_\text{DOD})^T \G \uht \in \R^{N} \qquad 
    \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal,
\end{equation*}
and then used to define the DOD-DL-ROM loss function utilizing the $\G$-orthonormality of the DOD, hence
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss function dod dl rom}
    \Lcal_\text{DOD-DL-ROM}\left(R_\rho(P(\Psi_{N'} \odot \Phi_n, \Psi'_n)(\theta)) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) =  \\
        \frac{1}{N_\text{data}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
        \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \bigg( \frac{\omega_h}{2} \, 
        \euknorm{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
        \left(R_\rho(\Psi_{N'}(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \\
        + \, \frac{1 - \omega_h}{2} \, \euknorm{R_\rho(\Psi'_n(\theta_\text{E}))(u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}) 
        - R_\rho(\Phi_n^\text{DF}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \bigg).
\end{multline}
Notice, that this reduction allows us to elect an algorithm, which uses only the $n$- and $N'$-dimensional euklidian norm,
therefore enabling even less computational complexity for each training epoch, under the assumption of an already 
optimized DOD.

\subsection{CoLoRA-DL-ROM}

\almostdone{Introduce CoLoRA DL-ROM}

Here we aim at minimizing the dimension through two major reductions; the first being the reduction of the stationary DOD to the latent dimension of the $\mu$-invariant solution manifold of the stationary equivalent to the problem (regarding the Dirichlet boundary conditions as the initial conditions on some a priori defined boundary), and the second concerning the actual reduction with CoLoRA-layered architecture to the latent dynamic dimension $n << N_h$. The general workflow is thus given by 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{u}_h(t; \mu, \nu, \theta^*_{sDOD}, \theta_{C}) = \A \cdot \mathcal{C}_L( \dots (\mathcal{C}_2(\mathcal{C}_1(V_0(\mu, \theta^*_{sDOD}) \cdot \phi_0(\mu, \nu, \theta^*_{sDOD})), \theta_{C_1}), \theta_{C_2}), \theta_{C_L}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_i: \R^{N_A} \times \Theta_C \to \R^{N_A}; \mathcal{C}_i(x, \theta_{C_i}) = W_i x + A_i \, \text{diag}(\alpha_i(\nu, t)) B_i x + b_i$ for all $1 \leq i \leq L.$
Remark, that we will still employ a pre-reduction using the rPOD matrix $\A$ stemming from the same snapshot matrix $S$ as in both Options before.
