\chapter{Formulation of the General Problem} \label{chapter: problem}


Let $\Theta \subset \R^{p}$ and $\Theta' \subset \R^q$ be compact and denote the geometric and physical parameter spaces 
respectively. 
Additionally, let $\Omega \subset \R^d$ be some Lipschitz domain and $[0, T]$ be a time interval
for some $T > 0$ of a parametric time-dependent PDE.
For $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\nu \in \Theta'$
\begin{equation*}
    u^{\mu, \nu} := u(\mu, \nu) , \quad \text{with sufficient regularity},
\end{equation*}
is called the (strong) solution of the PPDE, with the choice of the function space depending on the
specified problem, if
\begin{equation*}\begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t} u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) + \mathcal{L}(\mu, \nu)u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) + 
    \mathcal{N}(u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t), \mu, \nu) 
    = f(x, t; \mu, \nu), \quad &(x, t) \in \Omega \times [0, T], \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \mathcal{B}(\mu, \nu) u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) = g_N(x, t; \mu, \nu) &(x, t) \in \partial \Omega \times [0, T], \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    u^{\mu, \nu}(x, t) = u_0^{\mu, \nu}(x) &(x, t) \in \Omega \times \{0 \}, \\
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where $\mathcal{L}$ represents a linear, and $\mathcal{N}$ represents a non-linear integro-differential operator.
Using calculus of variation or distribution theory, 
one can then formulate a weak form of the problem and find a general space. For example, if the problem
has a weak form, the space commonly is
Sobolev (for elliptic problems with coercive properties), Bochner (for parabolic problems with coercive properties
on the stationary part) or discontinuous Galerkin spaces (for hyperbolic problems with enough regularity). 
Such a space $\Hcal$ is generally some Banach space, most often a subspace of
$L^2$.

This weak solution will have less regularity imposed than the strong formulation. Such weak form, then 
sometimes allows us to retrieve existence and uniqueness results. 
Quite famously, there are counter examples; the Navier-Stokes Equation in three 
dimensions poses one such result. For this PDE, a weak form exists, and numerical methods have been developed, which
can provide a solution in special cases, but existence remains unproven in general. 
As mentioned before, we will omit the transformation
of the strong PDE formulation into a weak one, and simple assume that this can be done.

Say we (can) discretize the problem in the spacial domain $\Omega$ with $N_h$ degrees of freedom. For this, 
let $\Hcal_h \subset \Hcal$ a Hilbert space with $\Hcal_h \cong \R^{N_h}$, an inner product 
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\Hcal_h}$ and a corresponding norm $\norm{\cdot}_{\Hcal_h}$.
Then the resulting full order model solution can be identified by
\begin{equation} \label{eq: fom solution identity}
    t \mapsto u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j = 1}^{N_h} [\uh(t)]_j \, \varphi_j(\mu) \in C^1([0, T]; \Hcal_h) 
\end{equation}
with $\{\varphi_j(\mu)\}_{j=1}^{N_h}$ a basis of $\Hcal_h$ for each $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\uh(t) \in \R^{N_h}$ 
for each $t \in [0, T]$. Then it is assumed, that the coefficients are a solution to a problem of the following
formulation.
\begin{equation} \label{eq: fom ppde}
    \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    M(\mu) \frac{\partial}{\partial{t}} \uh(t) + A(\mu, \nu) \uh(t) + 
    N(\uh(t), \mu, \nu)= f(t; \mu, \nu)  \quad &t \in [0, T], \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \uh(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t=0, \end{cases} 
\end{equation}
where we assume 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[$\bullet$] $M: \Theta \to \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ to send the geometrical parameter to a mass matrix,
    \item[$\bullet$] $A: \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h \times N_h}$ to send the set to a symmetric positive definite stiffness matrix,
    \item[$\bullet$] $N: \R^{N_h} \times \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h}$ to be some non-linear term,
    \item[$\bullet$] $f: [0, T] \times \R^{N_h} \times \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h}$ 
    to be the corresponding source term of the equation.
\end{enumerate}    
Additionally, let $u_0: \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R^{N_h}$ be the initial datum. 
The mass matrix is defined for each $\mu \in \Theta$ as follows
\begin{equation*}
    M(\mu) = \begin{bmatrix}
        \langle \varphi_1(\mu), \varphi_1(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h} & \cdots & \langle \varphi_1(\mu), \varphi_{N_h}(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h} \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        \langle \varphi_{N_h}(\mu), \varphi_1(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h} & \cdots & \langle \varphi_{N_h}(\mu), \varphi_{N_h}(\mu) \rangle_{\Hcal_h}
        \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
This allows us to equip $\Scal \subset \R^{N_h}$, the space of the coefficients 
of any solution in $\Hcal_h$, with the following
norm
\begin{equation} \label{eq: definition mass matrix norm}
    \norm{\uht} := \sqrt{(\uht)^T M(\mu) \uht}
\end{equation}
for $\mu \in \Theta, \nu \in \Theta', t \in [0, T]$ and $\uht \in \Scal$. In fact, 
the norm above used on the coefficient representation 
is equivalent to the $\Hcal_h$-norm, i.e. $\norm{\uht} = \norm{u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu)}_{\Hcal_h}$.

Note that it is indeed possible to achieve this discretization without skipping to a weak formulation, e.g.
using finite differences or finite volume schemes. 
For the interested reader, an explicit discretization using finite volume
schemes can be seen in \cite{haasdonk2008pod}.

The formulation in Equation (\ref{eq: fom ppde}) establishes smoothness with regard to time. This is called
\emph{method of lines approach} \cite[p. 278]{hesthaven2022rb}. 
To solve this resulted ODE, we will implicitly further discretize by using time steps
$\{t_k\}_{k=1}^{N_t} := \Tcal$ to gather data. 

\begin{definition}[Solution Data] \label{def: solution data}    
    We denote with
    \begin{equation*}
        \uht := \uh(t) \in \R^{N_h}, \quad \text{for }\mu \in \Theta, \nu \in \Theta' \text{ and all } t \in \Tcal,
    \end{equation*}
    a finite dimensional \emph{solution snapshot} and
    \begin{equation*}
        \uh := [\uh(t)]_{t \in \Tcal} \in \R^{N_h \times N_t}, 
        \quad \text{for }\mu \in \Theta \text{ and } \nu \in \Theta',
    \end{equation*}
    with $N_t := \vert \Tcal \vert$ a finite dimensional \emph{solution trajectory} of the discretized parametric PDE.
    Furthermore, let $\Pcal_1 \subset \Theta$, $\Pcal_2 \subset \Theta'$ and $\Pcal = \Pcal_1 \times
    \Pcal_2$ be some finite parameter data with sizes $N_{s_1}, N_{s_2} \in \N \cup \{\infty\}$ and $N_s = N_{s_1} N_{s_2}$, 
    respectively.
\end{definition}


We have stated the problem framework, 
to know which class
of PDEs, we will be considering. However, to justify the
use of $L^2$ norms, we assume that $\Hcal_h$ can be 
embedded in $L^2$. Then we can compare the finite dimensional solution $u_h \in \Hcal_h$ 
and the approximation of that solution $\hat{u}_h \in \Hcal_h$ (of reduced order) using ROM methods with $L^2$ estimates.

From this point forward, we want to consistently rely on the following few assumptions, which regulate the 
discrete problem above. 

Assumption (\ref{assumption KnW decay}) justifies a nuanced approach regarding the
geometric parameter $\mu$ and the physical parameter $\nu$, but can be relaxed
under some constraints. Note, that the exact size of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is not needed for any
of the upcoming proofs. However, the general separation of problems with slow and fast KnW decay is important
in the context of literature.

Assumption (\ref{assumption sample}) is required
for the derivation of any convergence result to a true parameter-to-solution map, 
when sampling an ever-growing number of examples. 

Assumption (\ref{assumption parameter-to-solution map})
imposes regularity on the mentioned parameter-to-solution map. It ensures
the existence of a true solution of the discrete problem for any specified parameter choice. We observe, that
this does not translate into the solvability for the upstream general problem.

Assumption (\ref{assumption perfect embedding}) gives us the ability to later meaningfully 
use an Autoencoder to achieve a reduction. Its application is justified with results from \cite{franco2023}.


\begin{assumption} \label{assumption KnW decay}
    Let $\mathcal{S} := \{ \uht \in \R^{N_h} \, \vert \, (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times [0, T] \}$ 
    denote the \emph{total solution manifold} and $\mathcal{S}_{\mu, t} := 
    \{ \uht \in \R^{N_h} \, \vert \, \nu \in \Theta' \}$ denote the \emph{solution 
    submanifold} for a fixed set $(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T]$, then we assume that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item the linear KnW of $\mathcal{S}$ decays slowly, i.e. 
        \begin{equation*}
            d_N(\mathcal{S}) \leq C N^{-\alpha},
        \end{equation*}
        where $0 < \alpha \leq 1$ and $C > 0$ some constant.
        \item the linear KnW of $\mathcal{S}_{\mu, t}$ decays quickly for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $t \in [0, T]$, i.e. 
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_{(\mu, t) \in \Theta \times [0, T]} d_N(\mathcal{S}_{\mu, t}) \leq C' N^{-\beta},    
        \end{equation*}
        where $\beta > 1$ and $C' > 0$ some constant.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

This is mostly satisfied for problems in which the transport dominated, or non-linear part is chained
to the parameter $\mu$, and
any coercive or elliptic operator is most heavily influence by $\nu$.

\begin{assumption} \label{assumption sample}    
    Let $\Theta, \Theta'$ and $[0, T]$
    for $T > 0$ as above. We assume that all $N_s \in \N_{\geq 2}$ parameter data points as in 
    (\ref{def: solution data}) are sampled 
    independent and identically distributed in the joint parameter space $\Theta \times \Theta'$.
    For the time variable a uniform grid 
    $\Tcal = \{\Delta t, 2 \Delta t, \dots, N_t \Delta t \}$ is used, 
    where $N_t \in \mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}$, $N_{s_1}$ and $N_{s_2}$ 
    are the respective number of random samples in the parameter spaces $\Theta$ and $\Theta'$,
    $N_s = N_{s_1} N_{s_2} \in \mathbb{N}$ is the total sample size, and $\Delta t = T / N_t$.
\end{assumption}

We remark, that the equidistant spacing of the time samples is a matter of convenience. 
It could be loosened under some constraints.

\begin{assumption} \label{assumption parameter-to-solution map}
    Let $\mathcal{G}: \Theta \times \Theta' \times [0, T] \to \R^{N_h}$ be the parameter-to-solution map, 
    mapping $(\mu, \nu, t) \mapsto \uht$. Then, we assume that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $m = 
        \underset{(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times \mathcal{T}}{\operatorname{ess}\, \inf}
        \norm{u_h(\mu, \nu, t)} > 0, \, M = 
        \underset{(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times \mathcal{T}}{\operatorname{ess}\, \sup}
        \norm{u_h(\mu, \nu, t)} < \infty$,
        \item $\mathcal{G}$ is Lipschitz-continuous with the constant $L > 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

There is another subliminal assumption inside the well-posed definition of such a parameter-to-solution map; the solution 
$u_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$ exists and is unique. This is meant, at least, in an $L^2$-embedded sense, 
since the time-derivative can be relaxed using a variational argument. 

\begin{assumption} \label{assumption perfect embedding}
    Let $N \in \N$ be fixed and $n \in \N$ such that $n \leq 2(p + q) + 3$. 
    We assume that for $s, s'$ sufficiently large, there are two maps 
    $\psi_*: \R^n \to \R^N$ and $\psi_*': \R^N \to \R^n$ 
    which attain a nonlinear embedding without loss of the reduced solution manifold, 
    as given in Definition (\ref{def: nonlinear kolmogorov}), i.e.
    for $\mathcal{S}_N := \{ q(\mu, \nu, t) = \A^T u(\mu, \nu, t) \, \vert \, (\mu, \nu, t) 
    \in \Theta \times \Theta' \times \Tcal \}$, where $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ is the POD matrix,
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta_n(\mathcal{S}_N) = 0.
    \end{equation*}
\end{assumption}



From here on, every introduced Reduced Order Model will pursue the following universal (and vague) goal.

\begin{goal}
    Find
    \begin{equation*}
        \hat{\mathcal{G}}: \Theta \times \Theta' \times [0, T] \to \R^{N_h}; \quad (\mu, \nu, t) \mapsto \uhthat,
    \end{equation*}
    such that for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\nu \in \Theta'$
    \begin{equation*}
        \sup\limits_{t \in [0, 1]}\norm{\uht - \uhthat} = 
        \sup\limits_{t \in [0, 1]}\norm{u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) - \hat{u}_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu)}_{\Hcal_h}
    \end{equation*}
    is small.
\end{goal}

We recall, that $\Hcal_h \subset \Hcal$ is a finite dimensional
approximation space of our weak problem. As mentioned before, we discretized time-steps.
Therefore, we have something akin to
\begin{equation} \label{eq: full discrete ppde}
    \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    M(\mu) \hat{\partial}_t \uh(t) + A(\mu, \nu) \uh(t) + 
    N(\uh(t), \mu, \nu)= f(t; \mu, \nu)  \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \uh(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t=0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
for $\hat{\partial}_t$ a discrete time-stepping operator and $\uh(t) \in \R^{N_h}$ for
$t \in [t_k, t_{k+1})$. For some $k = 0, \dots, N_t - 1$ the piecewise constant coefficient vector for the true solution
is then given by
\begin{equation*}
    u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j = 1}^{N_h} [\uh(t)]_j \, \varphi_j(\mu) \in \Hcal_h, 
    \text{ for each } t \in  [t_k, t_{k+1}) \text{ and } (\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'. 
\end{equation*}



\section{Intrusive Reduced Order Models}


In this section, we will introduce some \emph{intrusive} methods for the dimensionality reduction of the finite
dimensional parametric PDE. The name "intrusive" means to symbolize the "intrusion" of the method into the formulation
of the PDE and the specifications of its operators. There are methods for linear and non-linear
problems respectively. The more simple and lightweight reduction techniques can leverage an abstract linear variational
problem to achieve a (Petrov-)Galerkin projection. The methods for non-linear problems, so problems with $N \neq 0$
according to Equation (\ref{eq: fom ppde}), utilize the specifications of the non-linear operator to achieve reduction.

\begin{remark}[Historic Literature]
    The development of intrusive reduced order models (ROMs) dates back to the 
    late 1970s and 1980s, when methods for projection onto low-dimensional trial 
    spaces were systematically introduced in the context of parametrized PDEs. 
    Early formulations of the \emph{reduced basis (RB) method} can be found in the 
    control and approximation theory literature, e.g.\ in the pioneering works of 
    Courant and Hilbert on variational methods \cite{couranthilbert1953}, and in 
    the first computational implementations by Noor and coworkers 
    in structural mechanics \cite{noor1980}. These ideas were later rigorously 
    developed into the modern RB methodology by Patera in the mid-1980s 
    \cite{patera1984}, and subsequently advanced by Maday, Rovas, Prud'homme and 
    others in the 1990s and 2000s \cite{maday2002,prudhomme2002}.  

    In parallel, the POD emerged as a 
    computational tool for obtaining optimal low-rank approximations of 
    dynamical systems, building on statistical formulations dating back to 
    Karhunen and Loève. The introduction of POD-Galerkin for intrusive model 
    reduction was consolidated in applied mechanics and fluid dynamics in the 
    1990s and early 2000s (see \cite{holmes1996,kunischvolkwein2001}).  

    As nonlinear problems gained prominence, the cost of evaluating nonlinear terms 
    prompted the development of \emph{hyper-reduction techniques}, such as the 
    Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM) \cite{barrault2004} and its discrete 
    variants (DEIM) \cite{chaturantabut2010}, which remain core components of 
    modern intrusive ROM frameworks.  
\end{remark}


We will start with the framework of projection-based methods, that necessitate a problem with $N = 0$.
All the following methods will be discussed broadly, as the thesis will focus on the later data-driven ROMs. 

\begin{definition}[Reduced Basis]
    Let $\Hcal_n \subset \operatorname{span}(u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) \in \Hcal_h \, \vert \,
    (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal)$ be an $n$-dimensional subspace
    of $\Hcal_h$, then if $\{\hat{\varphi}_j\}_{j=1}^n$ is an orthonormalized basis of $\Hcal_n$, we call this the
    \emph{reduced basis}.
\end{definition}

Henceforth, the goal of all \emph{reduced basis methods} is to find a reduced basis $\{\hat{\varphi}_j(\mu)\}_{j=1}^n$ 
and $\uhhat(t) \in \R^n$ for $n \ll N_h$, such that
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{u}_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} [\uhhat(t)]_j \, \hat{\varphi}_j(\mu) \in \Hcal_n \subset \Hcal_h.
\end{equation*}
Note, that we aim to vaguely achieve
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{u}_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) \approx u_h(\cdot, t; \mu, \nu) \quad \text{for all } \mu \in \Theta, \nu \in \Theta'
    \text{ and } t \in [0, T].
\end{equation*}
In the terminology the Reduced Basis (RB) methods are a setup for many ROMs, and often appear for so-called
projection-based ROMs. These itself are 
intrusive ROMs. Projection-based ROMs identify a reduced basis, and then solve the reduced problem
for the coefficients of this basis.
Two prominent examples of such RB methods in the context of projection-based ROMs can be found in the following. 
Both will contain intrinsically an \emph{offline}
and an \emph{online} part in the algorithm, whose separation we will mostly neglect. However, 
the offline procedure commonly refers to the part of the algorithm, that can be collected a priori to 
its purposed deployment. Here, this is the construction of a reduced basis via samples. 
On the other hand, online refers to its counterpart; the many-query element of the procedure, such as the evaluation of the 
best-guess parameter-to-solution map. This terminology is used frequently throughout the literature as it instinctively 
makes sense in a software development environment.


\subsection{Galerkin Proper Orthogonal Decomposition}

As a prerequisite for Galerkin orthogonality, we assume for this subsection that the weak form 
giving rise to the equation at hand derives
from an abstract bilinear form in the stationary part. This is justified, since the non-linear summand is assumed to be zero. 
The source for this subsection is \cite[p. 137]{quarteroni2015rb}.

The POD as introduced in Definition (\ref{def: POD}) is one of the most common methods for a dimensional reduction. 
We can use the system above to solve for the solution using a Galerkin projection for the following
identity.
\begin{equation} \label{eq: identity for pod galerkin}
    \hat{u}_h(x, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j=1}^n [\uhhat(t)]_j \, \xi_j(x),
\end{equation}
where $x \in \Omega$, $t \in [0, T]$, $(\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'$ and 
$\xi_j$ for $1 \leq j \leq n$ are the POD modes, of Definition 
(\ref{def: POD}) with $\G := \I_{N_h}$. Then $\uhhat(t) \in \R^n$
is the coefficient vector, that solves the reduced equation
\begin{equation} \label{eq: reduced full discrete ppde}
    \begin{cases}
        \A^T M(\mu) \A \hat{\partial}_t \uhhat(t) + \A^T A(\mu, \nu) \A \uhhat(t)
        = \A^T f(t; \mu, \nu) \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\
        \uhhat(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t = 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Note, that in this construction $\A$ is generally \emph{not} $M(\mu)$-orthogonal. However, if we can set up the
problem with a $\mu$-independent mass matrix $\G$, then such a POD matrix would retain $\G$-orthonormality. This would then
conclude in a simpler variant of the problem in Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde}) due to $\A^T \G \A = \I_n$.
The parameter-to-solution map can be recovered by the following Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod-galerkin}), where the steps
1--4 are executed \emph{offline} and 5--6 are called for each \emph{online} computation.

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{POD-Galerkin} \label{algo: pod-galerkin}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $\left(\mu_j, \nu_j\right)_{j=1}^{N_s}$ data sample
        and $n < N_h$ reduced dimension
        \Ensure approximated parameter-to-solution-map $(\mu, \nu) \mapsto 
        \hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$
        \State Compute $\{u_h^{\mu_j, \nu_j, t_{k_m}}\}_{m=1}^{N_t}$ from 
        Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}) (with $N = 0$)
        \State $(\xi_j, \sigma_j)_{j=1}^n \gets \text{POD}(u_h^{\mu_j,\nu_j,t_{k_m}}, n)$
        \State $\A \gets \text{stack}(\xi_j, j=1, \dots, n)$
        \State Build reduced Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde}) 
        \State For each $(\mu, \nu)$ solve Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde})
        \State Recover $\hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$ via Equation (\ref{eq: identity for pod galerkin})
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}





\subsection{Greedy Proper Orthogonal Decomposition}





An introduction to this method in the stationary and well-posed case can be found in \cite{siena2022pod}. As before, 
we assume $N = 0$ in Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}) and the inheritance
from a weak formulation. Additionally, let $\eta : \Theta \times \Theta' \to \R$ be 
a function estimating the error of projection under a specific parameter set, i.e. 
\begin{equation*}
    \sup\limits_{t \in \Tcal} \, \norm{u_h(\mu, \nu, t) - \hat{u}_h(\mu, \nu, t)} \leq \eta(\mu, \nu),
\end{equation*}
for $(\mu, \nu) \in \Pcal$. Purely using POD-Galerkin can encounter major pitfalls. Namely, if the range of $\Pcal$ is extensive, 
the calculation of an eigenvalue decomposition for the correlation matrix might get too cumbersome to provide all
areas of interest in the set. Moreover, to obtain the snapshots for fixed parameters, a complete time-trajectory must
be computed, which entails a lot of overhead of otherwise useless information. To overcome this the mixed POD-Greedy approach 
was proposed in \cite{grepl2005rb}. This method was later improved in \cite{haasdonk2008pod}. The method 
takes a POD over the solution trajectories
(or, in the improved version, the error trajectories), while using the Greedy approach to handle the parameter samples.
It follows a similar workflow, as the POD-Galerkin, since the only thing, that changes is the choice and number of basis functions.
In fact, the size of the basis is chosen according to the tolerance in the offline part of the algorithm.

Let
\begin{equation} \label{eq: identity for pod greedy galerkin}
    \hat{u}_h(x, t; \mu, \nu) = \sum_{j=1}^N [\uhhat(t)]_j \, \xi_j(x),
\end{equation}
with $x \in \Omega$, $t \in [0, 1]$, $(\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'$ 
identify the reduced solution of dimension $N$, where $\xi_j$ are the basis functions obtained from the offline procedure
in Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod-greedy-galerkin}) for $1 \leq j \leq N$. Then $\uhhat(t) \in \R^N$ is the
coefficient vector, that solves the reduced equation
\begin{equation} \label{eq: reduced full discrete ppde greedy}
    \begin{cases}
        \V_\text{rb}^T M(\mu) \V_\text{rb} \hat{\partial}_t \uhhat(t) + \V_\text{rb}^T A(\mu, \nu) \V_\text{rb} \uhhat(t)
        = \V_\text{rb}^T f(t; \mu, \nu) \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\
        \uhhat(0) = u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t = 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Note again, that $\V_\text{rb}$ is generally \emph{not} $M(\mu)$-orthonormal.
The complexity of the offline computation for this Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod-greedy-galerkin}) is additive, 
and not multiplicative, in the number of 
training data $\Pcal$. This can be an advantage, when compared to the pure POD-Galerkin approach. 

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{POD-Greedy-Galerkin} \label{algo: pod-greedy-galerkin}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require \texttt{tol}, $\mathcal{Z} = 0$, $N = 0$, $(\mu_j, \nu_j)_{j=1}^{N_s}$, $N_1$ and $N_2$
        \Ensure approximated parameter-to-solution-map $(\mu, \nu) \mapsto 
        \hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$
        \For{$j = 1,\dots,N_s$}
            \State Compute $\{u_h^{\mu_j, \nu_j, t_{k_m}}\}_{m=1}^{N_t}$ from 
            Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}) (with $N = 0$)
            \State $(\zeta_j, \lambda_j)_{j=1}^{N_1} \gets 
            \text{POD}(\{u_h^{\mu_j,\nu_j,t_{k_m}}\}_{m=1}^{N_t}, N_1)$
            \State $\mathcal{Z} \gets \{\mathcal{Z}, \{\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_{N_1}\}\}$
            \State $N \gets N + N_2$ 
            \State Compress: $(\xi_j, \sigma_j)_{j=1}^n \gets \text{POD}(\mathcal{Z}, N)$
            \State $(\mu_{j+1}, \nu_{j+1}) \gets \underset{(\mu, \nu) \in \Pcal}{\operatorname{arg\,max}} \, \eta(\mu, \nu)$
            \If{$\eta(\mu_{j+1}, \nu_{j+1}) > \texttt{tol}$}
                \State $j \gets j + 1$
            \Else
                \State \textbf{break}
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State $\V_\text{rb} \gets \text{stack}(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N)$
        \State Build reduced Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde greedy}) 
        \State For each $(\mu, \nu)$ solve Equation (\ref{eq: reduced full discrete ppde greedy})
        \State Recover $\hat{u}_h(\cdot, \cdot \, ; \mu, \nu)$ via Equation (\ref{eq: identity for pod greedy galerkin})
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}





\subsection{Non-linear Reduced Order Models}




In this subsection, we want to briefly introduce relevant ROMs, that handle situations in which $N \neq 0$
according to Equation (\ref{eq: fom ppde}). For discrete problem derived from Finite Element Methods, 
such situations can be found, in case
the associated weak form of the PPDE has a non-affine representation in the respective space in regard
to the parameters. Each of these methods, try to overcome the issue of $N_h$-dimensional workflow in 
the $N$ operator. This is straightforward, if one would use a RB-method in order to substitute
the Equation (\ref{eq: fom ppde}) in the same way as we have done in projection-based ROMs above.
In such case, we would get
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{cases}
        M_\text{red}(\mu) \hat{\partial}_t u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu}(t) + A_\text{red}(\mu, \nu) u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu}(t)
        + V^T N(V u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu}(t), \mu, \nu)= f_\text{red}(t; \mu, \nu) \quad &t \in \Tcal, \\
        u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu}(0) = V^T u_0(\mu, \nu) \quad &t = 0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
with $\uhhat(t) \approx V u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu}(t)$ for each $t \in [0, T]$ and some reduction operator
$V$. Hence, we would still rely on 
the high dimensional evaluation of the non-linear term.
In the following, we will list some of the intrusive ROMs to overcome such evaluating, and
give a brief description of their workflow.

\paragraph{Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM)}

In essence, the EIM leverages on interpolation functionals, which are defined iteratively.
We minimize with regard to $\inf_{z \in W_{M-1}} \norm{N(\cdot, \mu, \nu) - z}_{L^\infty(\Omega)}$,
where $W_{M-1}$ is the span of the last iteration, which appends all former ones. The start
of this iteration is given by setting $W_1 := \operatorname{span}(\xi_1)$ with $\xi_1 := 
N(u_h^{\mu_1, \nu_1, t_1}, \mu, \nu)$ for some solution snapshot.
This constitutes finding a space, in which the non-affine (non-linear) operator $N$ is approximated well
by interpolation. After sufficient steps, we stop the creation of this space and take the wanted
amount $m < N_h$ of interpolation functionals spanning that space. 
Then, we approximate with $U = [\xi_j]_{j=1}^m$ and $P \in \R^{N_h \times m}$ being the selection matrix for the
non-linear term under a less costly interpolant
\begin{equation*}
    N(\uhhat, \mu, \nu) \approx U (P^T U)^{-1} P^T N(\uhthat, \mu, \nu).
\end{equation*}
In fact, the expensive $U (P^T U)^{-1}$ term can be computed offline, 
while only $m \ll N_h$ entries of $N(\uhhat, \mu, \nu)$ need to be assembled online. The EIM has been
introduced in \cite{barrault2004}, but a more fitting definition for our framework can be found
in \cite{quarteroni2015rb}.

\paragraph{Gappy-POD Residual Minimization (GNAT)}

For simplicity and consistency with the literature's notation, we will denote with $R: \R^{N_h} \to \R^{N_h}$
given by
\begin{equation*}
    R(u) := M(\mu) \partial_t u + A(\mu, \nu) + N(u, \mu, \nu) - f(t; \mu, \nu)
\end{equation*}
the residual of the problem in Equation (\ref{eq: fom ppde}). The GNAT aims at reducing the dimensionality
of an arbitrary residual realization by using a Petrov-Galerkin projection. Thus, it limits the domain of the
residual to a less complex, intrinsically low dimensional, subspace of $\R^{N_h}$, by using
gappy POD and sampling. After this, it
solves a least-squares problem with a Gauss-Newton method, i.e. it finds
\begin{equation*}
    \min\limits_{u_\text{red}} \norm{P^T R(V u_\text{red})}^2,
\end{equation*}
with $P \in \R^{N_h \times m}$ a basis selector and $V$ the reduction matrix.
For this refer to \cite{carlberg2013}.
However, the origin of the method can be found in \cite{carlberg2011}.







\section{Data-driven Reduced Order Models} \label{section: data-driven ROMs}




In contrast to the introduced intrusive ROMs above, the \emph{data-driven} alternatives aim to circumvent 
the direct use of the governing equations. As such, they allow a more general treatment.

\begin{remark}[Historic Literature]
    The origins of data-driven ROMs can be traced back to techniques in fluid 
    dynamics and control, where the objective was to extract coherent structures 
    or dominant modes from experimental measurements without explicit reliance on 
    the full Navier-Stokes equations. A first influential step was the development 
    of the POD for experimental data, 
    introduced in the turbulence community by Lumley \cite{lumley1967}, and further 
    popularized in the coherent structure analysis of Sirovich \cite{sirovich1987}. 
    While the POD itself does not provide a dynamical law for the online phase, it laid the foundation for 
    representing complex systems with only few empirical modes.

    A decisive breakthrough was the 
    \emph{Dynamic Mode Decomposition} (DMD) \cite{schmid2010}, which can be 
    interpreted as a data-driven approximation of the infinite-dimensional Koopman 
    operator \cite{rowley2009}. DMD represents temporal evolution as a best-fit 
    linear operator acting on snapshot data, thereby yielding both modal 
    decompositions and a surrogate model for prediction. This was one of the first 
    systematic attempts to construct a fully non-intrusive ROM.

    In parallel, regression-based approaches have been developed to learn reduced 
    operators directly from data, often under the name of 
    \emph{operator inference} \cite{peherstorfer2016}, or in the form of 
    interpolation-based reduced models, e.g.\ POD with interpolation (PODI) 
    as in Barrault et al. \cite{barrault2004}. These methods demonstrate that the 
    reduced dynamics can be inferred without Galerkin projection, either by 
    statistical regression or by interpolation in parameter space.
\end{remark}

For this section we intentionally do not give an explicit training algorithm for any of the methods presented,
since it can be inferred with an optimizer of the readers choice from the discussion 
in Subsection (\ref{subsec: statistical learning}). With readability in mind, we thus only state the architecture
of the network at hand and its loss function. Most importantly, we assume that some choice of basis
for $\Hcal_h$ is already done a priori to the training of the network and independent of $\mu \in \Theta$.
Thus, we assume $\G := M(\mu)$ for all $\mu \in \Theta$ as our mass matrix. Under Equation
(\ref{eq: definition mass matrix norm}) this also implies a $\mu$-independent norm $\norm{\cdot}$, which in fact
allow us to use the POD matrix, if constructed with $\G$, as a $\G$-orthogonal projector 
onto the span of $\A$ in an $L^2$ sense.
Note, that this could be relaxed, if a general equivalent norm would be known without imposing a $\mu$-independent
basis.

For clarity, we formulate the following as an assumption, since the reduction systems presented
can choose these dimensional numbers somewhat freely. They should however retain this hierarchy, to ensure comparability.
Note, that the number of systems can make the dimensionality of the architectures confusing.
The reader is therefore encouraged to revisit this assumption, whenever
the general setting appears to be unclear.

\begin{assumption}[Dimension Hierarchy] \label{assumption: dimension hierarchy}
    Let for all following data-driven Reduced Order Models, the following natural number hierarchy be fixed
    \begin{equation*}
        n < N' < N \ll N_A \leq N_h.
    \end{equation*}
    Note, that "$\ll$" is an unspecified relation, meaning to symbolize a "large" difference.
\end{assumption}

A general Deep Learning based ROM (DL-ROM) first appeared in architecture and 
training in the paper \cite{fresca2020}. To properly approximate the potentially 
non-linear solution manifold of the high fidelity FOM, an Autoencoder (AE) is deployed to map the degrees of freedom 
to a coefficient dimension, 
from where some other neural network architecture, 
like the proposed Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network (DFNN), is used to learn the latent dynamics of the parametric PDE.
This concludes in the following approximation of the FOM coefficient solution $\uht \in \R^{N_h}$ for each 
$\mu \in \Theta$, $\nu \in \Theta'$ and $t \in \Tcal$
\begin{equation} \label{eq: dl rom solution}
    \uhthat = \left(R_\rho(\Psi_h) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n)\right) (t; \mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h},
\end{equation}
where we set $\Psi_h = f^\text{D}_h$ for the decoder architecture with input dimension $n$ and output dimension $N_h$
(and $\Psi'_n = f^\text{E}_n$ the corresponding encoder architecture) of a convolutional AE. 
Then $\Psi'_n$ denotes the projection onto the reduced trial solution manifold, and we 
let $\Phi_n = \Phi_n^\text{DF}$  
to be the DFNN architecture of input dimension $p + q + 1$ and output dimension $n$, 
mapping the dynamics of the parametric PDE onto the reduced trial solution manifold.

The subsequent training is then performed via a loss function of a convex combination of both, 
the projection error using the encoder and the dynamics error considering both the decoder and 
the feed forward network. For this optimization problem, we define 
the trainable weights $\theta = (\theta_\text{D}, \theta_\text{E}, \theta_\text{DF}) 
\in \Theta(\Psi_h) \times \Theta(\Psi_n') \times \Theta(\Phi_n)$ and the loss function
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss function dl rom}
    \Lcal_\text{DL-ROM}\left(R_\rho(P(\Psi_h \odot \Phi_n, \Psi_n')(\theta)) \vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_h^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) =  \\
        \sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
        \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \frac{\omega_h}{2} \, 
        \norm{u_h^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
        \left(R_\rho(\Psi_h(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \\
        + \, \frac{1 - \omega_h}{2} \, \euknorm{R_\rho(\Psi'_n(\theta_\text{E}))(u_h^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}) 
        - R_\rho(\Phi_n^\text{DF}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2.
\end{multline}





\subsection{Proper Orthogonal Decomposition Deep Learning ROM} \label{subsec: pod-dl-rom}




The main idea of the POD-DL-ROM, in contrast to the DL-ROM, is the additional employment of an 
outer a priori reduction of the training data using a POD. We base the following definition on \cite{fresca2022}. 
Note, that we
sway somewhat from the source, since we are trying to achieve 
an estimate, which is close to the high-fidelity solution
in an $L^2$-sense by applying the
mass matrix $\G$ from the start of the section.
Such a reduction yields great benefits, such as the scalability in $N_h$ 
and reduction in computational cost for
the offline training. This is done by employing a POD (see Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod}))
on the stacked solution trajectories 
\begin{equation*}
    U := \left[u_h^{\mu_1, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert 
    u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_{N_{s_2}}}\right]^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}},
\end{equation*}
of Definition (\ref{def: solution data}), resulting in the POD matrix 
$\A_\text{P} \in \R^{N_h \times N}$ for $N < N_h$ from Assumption (\ref{assumption: dimension hierarchy}). Then we can
define the reduced solution snapshots by computing
\begin{equation*}
    u_\text{red}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \A_\text{P} ^T \G \uht \in \R^{N} \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
By $\G$-orthonormality of $\A_\text{P} $, we can thus define the FOM approximate coefficient solution $\uhthat \in \R^{N_h}$ for each 
$\mu \in \Theta$, $\nu \in \Theta'$ and $t \in \Tcal$
\begin{equation} \label{eq: pod-dl-rom solution}
    \uhthat = \A_\text{P}  \cdot \left(R_\rho(\Psi_N) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n)\right) (t; \mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h},
\end{equation}
where we set $\Psi_N = f^\text{D}_{N}$ for the decoder architecture with input dimension $n$ and output dimension $N$
(and $\Psi'_n = f^\text{E}_n$ the corresponding encoder architecture) of a convolutional AE. 
Then $f^\text{E}_n$ denotes the projection onto the reduced trial solution manifold, and we 
let $\Phi_n = \Phi_n^\text{DF}$  
be the DFNN architecture of input dimension $p + q + 1$ and output dimension $n$ same as before.

The loss function has now the attribute of being fed only at most $N$-dimensional training data, and only
evaluating at most $N$-dimensional euclidean norms, hence greatly 
increasing performance. The justification of using the euclidean norm here
comes directly from the Proposition (\ref{prop: switch to euclidean norm}),
where the averaging over all deterministic POD projection errors can be collectively ignored for training,
without misrepresenting the solution as a bare vector in euclidean space.

Hence, we can define the optimization problem. Let the neural network weights $\theta = 
(\theta_\text{D}, \theta_\text{E}, \theta_\text{DF}) 
\in \Theta(\Psi_N) \times \Theta(\Psi_n') \times \Theta(\Phi_n)$ and the loss function be defined by
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss function pod dl rom}
    \Lcal_\text{POD-DL-ROM}\left(R_\rho(P(\Psi_N \odot \Phi_n, \Psi'_n)(\theta)) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_\text{red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) =  \\
        \frac{1}{N_\text{data}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
        \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \bigg( \frac{\omega_h}{2} \, 
        \euknorm{u_\text{red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
        \left(R_\rho(\Psi_N(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \\
        + \, \frac{1 - \omega_h}{2} \, \euknorm{R_\rho(\Psi'_n(\theta_\text{E}))(u_\text{red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}) 
        - R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \bigg).
\end{multline}



\subsection{Deep Orthogonal Decomposition based ROMs} \label{subsec: dod-based roms}





A big advantage of the POD-DL-ROM is the fact, that under the assumption of a static basis 
$\{\xi_j\}_{j=1}^{N_h} \subset \Hcal_h$ of $\Hcal_h$ and its associated mass matrix $\G$ and $\Hcal_h$ sensitive norm
$\norm{\cdot}$, we may reduce the high dimensional optimization problem to a more manageable
computational complexity.
However, this so far ignored the particular problem in sight. Assumption (\ref{assumption KnW decay})
guarantees a significant reduction cost, when employing a $(\mu, t)$-independent linear projector.
To circumvent this Kolmogorov $N$-width barrier for $N \ll N_h$, one can instead of using a POD for the choice of reduced basis,
invoke a Neural Network based linear projector, that fits a basis superposition dependent on $\mu$ and $t$.
This projector can thus produce a further reduction to $N' < N$ under a similar energy loss.

The following depicts an adaptive RB method. 
\begin{definition}[Deep Orthogonal Decomposition] \label{def: DOD}
    Recollect the dimensional hierarchy of Assumption (\ref{assumption: dimension hierarchy}).
    Employ a POD (according to Algorithm (\ref{algo: pod})) with $\G$
    given as above, using
    the stacked solution trajectories
    \begin{equation*}
        U := \left[u_h^{\mu_1, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert 
        u_h^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_{N_{s_2}}}\right]^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_\text{data}}.
    \end{equation*}
    This results in the POD matrix $\A \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$.
    Now set up a Neural Network architecture 
    $\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}$ with input dimension $p + 1$ and output dimension $N_A \times N'$,
    such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi_{\tilde{\V}} = \text{ORTH} \circ \text{STACK} \circ (P((R_1 \odot s), \dots, (R_N \odot s))),
    \end{equation*}
    where $P$ is denoting the network parallelization operator as given in Definition (\ref{def: network parallelization}).
    Here, we have
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $s$ is a Deep Feed Forward Neural Network architecture with input dimension $p + 1$ and output dimension $l$,
        \item $R_1, \dots, R_N$ are a collection of Deep Feed Forward Neural Network architectures
        with input dimensions $l$ and output dimensions $N_A$,
        \item $\text{STACK}: \R^{N_A \cdot N'} \to \R^{N_A \times N'}$ is a canonical stacking operator, 
        \item $\text{ORTH}: \R^{N_A \times N'} \to \R^{N_A \times N'}$ is an orthonormalization unit, that orthonormalizes
        any given matrix $W \in \R^{N_A \times N'}$ into $\tilde{W} \in \R^{N_A \times N'}$ such that 
        $\text{span}(W) = \text{span}(\tilde W)$. This is most often done using a QR decomposition \cite{golubvanloan2013}. 
    \end{enumerate}
    The realization of the network $\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}$ for some $\mu \in \Theta$, $t \in [0, T]$ and
    any chosen neural network weight $\theta_\text{DOD} \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ 
    according to Definition (\ref{def: neural network weights}) will be abbreviated with
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: inner module DOD}
        \tilde{\V}_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) := R_\rho(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}(\theta_\text{DOD}))(\mu, t).
    \end{equation}
    For any choice of weights, this is called the \emph{inner module of the Deep Orthogonal Decomposition}, or in short 
    the inner DOD module.

    \emph{The Deep Orthogonal Decomposition} can hence be achieved by upward projection under the POD matrix $\A$ of 
    the inner DOD module. In other words, a realization of the underlying DOD neural network for a choice of weights
    $\theta_\text{DOD} \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ is attained for
    $\mu \in \Theta$ and $t \in [0, T]$ by
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: DOD}
        \V_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) := \A \cdot \tilde{\V}_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) \in \R^{N_h \times N'}.
    \end{equation}
    This is visualized in Figure (\ref{fig:dod-matrix-workflow}).
\end{definition}

Nonetheless, this does not warrant a "good" DOD; just a defined one. 
For this one needs to assert a qualitative loss function and some data, which we will collect by pre-reduction
in the same manner as before, i.e. let
\begin{equation*}
    u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \A^T \G \uht \in \R^{N_A} \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
Then, we define the optimization problem as follows. Let $\theta = \theta_\text{DOD} 
\in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ be the trainable weights, then we optimize using the following loss function
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss dod}
    \Lcal_\text{DOD}\left(R_\rho(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}(\theta)) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) \\
    = \frac{1}{N_\text{data}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
    \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \euknorm{u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
    \tilde{\V}_{\mu_i, k \Delta t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) \tilde{\V}^T_{\mu_i, k \Delta t}(\theta_\text{DOD}) 
    u_\text{pre-red}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}}^2.
\end{multline}
Note, that this is indeed again an $N_A$-dimensional loss function, thanks to the $\G$-orthonormality
of the POD matrix and Proposition (\ref{prop: switch to euclidean norm}).
We will denote by $\theta_\text{DOD}^* \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ the optimized neural network weights.

Notice, that we have not yet performed any actual search for the approximate parameter-to-solution map $\Gcal$. 
After all, the frozen DOD is a linear orthogonal projector, and not an estimation of $\Gcal$. It can be seen
as an analog to the POD.

In this paper, we propose two novel data-driven ROMs, based on the DOD, to find an approximate solution-to-parameter 
map. These are the \emph{Deep Orthogonal Decomposition with Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network} (DOD+DFNN) and the
\emph{Deep Orthogonal Decomposition based Deep Learning ROM} (DOD-DL-ROM). The former indeed symbolizes a general 
approach hence including the latter, but, with some abuse of notation, we admit the DOD+DFNN a naive direct
Deep Feed Forward architecture for the determination of the coefficient, while the DOD-DL-ROM is an adaptation
of the DL-ROM architecture introduced in the header of the section.

\subsubsection{DOD+DFNN} \label{subsec: DOD+DFNN}

Let $\Phi_{N'} := \Phi_{N'}^\text{DF}$ 
be some Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network architecture
with input dimension $p+q+1$ and output dimension $N'$. Then the approximate parameter-to-solution map 
of the \emph{DOD+DFNN}
can be given for each $(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta'
\times [0, T]$ via
\begin{equation} \label{eq: dod+dnn solution}
    \uhthat = \A \cdot R_\rho(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})(\mu, t) \cdot R_\rho(\Phi_{N'})(\mu, \nu, t) \in \R^{N_h}.
\end{equation}

The training is done in series and not in parallel.
We first find the optimal DOD parameter $\theta^*_\text{DOD} \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ by 
solving the optimization problem
in Definition (\ref{def: DOD}). This freezes the DOD, and we set $\V_{\mu, t} := \V_{\mu, t}(\theta^*_\text{DOD})$.

Now, we can use the frozen DOD matrix to reduce the training data
\begin{equation*}
    u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \V_{\mu, t}^T \G \uht \in \R^{N'} 
    \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
Let the optimization problem be given for any choice of neural network weights $\theta = \theta_\text{DF} \in \Theta(\Phi_{N'})$
by the loss function
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss dod+dnn}
    \Lcal_\text{DOD+DNN}\left(R_\rho(\Phi_{N'}(\theta_\text{DF})) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) \\
    = \frac{1}{N_\text{data}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
    \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \euknorm{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
    R_\rho(\Phi_{N'}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2.
\end{multline}

This is a rather obvious extension of \cite{franco2024}, but works surprisingly well. 
It will be the basic benchmark to overcome for the following algorithms. 





\subsubsection{DOD-DL-ROM} \label{subsec: dod-dl-rom}





Let $\Psi_{N'} := f_{N'}^\text{D}$ be a Decoder architecture with input dimension $n$ and output dimension $N'$ 
(and $\Psi'_n := f_n^\text{E}$ the corresponding Encoder architecture), and $\Phi_n := \Phi_n^\text{DF}$
be some Deep Feed-Forward Neural Network architecture with input dimension $p+q+1$ and output dimension $n$.
Then the approximate parameter-to-solution map of the \emph{DOD-DL-ROM} 
can be given for each $(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Theta \times \Theta'
\times [0, T]$ via
\begin{equation} \label{eq: dod-dl-rom solution}
    \uhthat = \A \cdot R_\rho(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}) \cdot 
    \left(R_\rho(\Psi_{N'}) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n)\right) (t; \mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h}.
\end{equation}

Same as for the DOD+DNN, the training is done in series. We find the optimal DOD parameter 
$\theta^*_\text{DOD} \in \Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}})$ by solving the optimization problem in
Definition (\ref{def: DOD}). This again freezes the DOD, and we set $\V_{\mu, t}:= \V_{\mu, t}(\theta_\text{DOD}^*)$.
One then observes, that the training data can be again
reduced with
\begin{equation*}
    u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu, \nu, t} := \V_{\mu, t}^T \G \uht \in \R^{N'} \qquad 
    \text{for all } (\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
Let the optimization problem be given for any choice of neural network weights $\theta = 
(\theta_\text{DOD}, \theta_\text{D}, \theta_\text{DF}) \in 
\Theta(\Phi_{\tilde{\V}}) \times \Theta(f_N^\text{D}) \times \Theta(\Phi_n^\text{DF})$
by the loss function
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss function dod dl rom}
    \Lcal_\text{DOD-DL-ROM}\left(R_\rho(P(\Psi_{N'} \odot \Phi_n, \Psi'_n)(\theta)) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) =  \\
        \frac{1}{N_\text{data}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
        \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \bigg( \frac{\omega_h}{2} \, 
        \euknorm{u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
        \left(R_\rho(\Psi_{N'}(\theta_\text{D})) \circ R_\rho(\Phi_n(\theta_\text{DF}))\right) (\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \\
        + \, \frac{1 - \omega_h}{2} \, \euknorm{R_\rho(\Psi'_n(\theta_\text{E}))(u_{\text{red}^2}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t}) 
        - R_\rho(\Phi_n^\text{DF}(\theta_\text{DF}))(\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t)}^2 \bigg).
\end{multline}
Notice, that this reduction allows us to elect an algorithm, which uses only the $n$- and $N'$-dimensional euclidean norm,
therefore enabling even less computational complexity for each training epoch, under the assumption of an already 
optimized DOD.



\subsection{A CoLoRA inspired approach}




In this subsection, we will introduce a novel 
architecture based on the Continuous Low Rank Adaptation (CoLoRA) \cite{berman2024}.
It will use the stationary version of the Deep Orthogonal Decomposition and a supplementing
Dense Forward Neural Network, defined in \cite{franco2024}. This will be done to achieve a solution approximation of the 
stationary limit case of the problem. This "first guess" is then continuously adapted during the online phase using CoLoRA layers.
Notice, that such a procedure only makes sense in a case, where the problem has a meaningful stationary limit case.

For this, let for all $\mu \in \Theta$ and $\nu \in \Theta'$
\begin{equation} \label{eq: full discrete stationary ppde}
    \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle
    A(\mu, \nu) \statuh + 
    N(\statuh, \mu, \nu)= 0\\[0.8em]
    \displaystyle
    \statuh = u_{D}^{\mu, \nu} \quad \text{on some boundary}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
define a fully discretized stationary problem. Note, that the boundary term could also be of some other type like
Neumann or Robin. We assume, that this problem is 
closely related to the time-dependent version, in a sense that
\begin{equation*}
    \lim\limits_{t \to T} \uht = \statuh, \qquad \text{for all } (\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'.
\end{equation*}
Then we define the stationary model using the DOD Definition (\ref{def: DOD}) with $[0, T] = \{T\}$.
The pre-reduction here is taken as a POD $\A_0 \in \R^{N_h \times N_A}$ of the stationary snapshot matrix
\begin{equation*}
    U_0 = \left[u_{h, 0}^{\mu_1, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert u_{h, 0}^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_1} \big\vert \cdots \big\vert 
    u_{h, 0}^{\mu_{N_{s_1}}, \nu_{N_{s_2}}}\right]^T \in \R^{N_h \times N_s}.
\end{equation*}
We can then produce some stationary reduced data $u_{\text{pre-red}, 0}^{\mu, \nu} := \A_0^T \G \statuh \in \R^{N_A}$ for $\mu \in \Pcal_1$
and $\nu \in \Pcal_2$, where $\statuh \in \R^{N_h}$ are the solutions of Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete stationary ppde}).
This data is used to find the optimal neural network weights $\theta^*_\text{sDOD} \in \Theta(\Phi^\text{stat}_{\tilde{\V}})$ 
for the stationary DOD, thus freezing it. 
We then denote with $\tilde{\V}_\mu := R_\rho(\Phi^\text{stat}_{\tilde{\V}}(\theta^*_\text{sDOD}))(\mu)$ 
any realization of the inner
stationary DOD module as given in \cite{franco2024}.
As a next step, we define a DFNN $\Phi^\text{stat}_{N'}$ in an analogous way to the DOD+DFNN subsection above, 
to attain for any 
input choice $(\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'$ the total approximation for a stationary solution of the form
\begin{equation*}
    \statuhhat =  \A_0 \cdot \tilde{\V}_{\mu} \cdot 
    R_\rho(\Phi^\text{stat}_{N'})(\mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_h}.
\end{equation*}
Then let the neural network weights $\theta_\text{sDFNN} \in \Theta(\Phi^\text{stat}_{N'})$ and the loss
function of the corresponding optimization problem be given in an analogous way to the DOD+DFNN.
Solving it, will freeze the stationary DFNN as well. This lets us write the frozen stationary approximation
for the optimal 
weights $\theta^*_\text{sDFNN} \in \Theta(\Phi^\text{stat}_{N'})$ as
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{u}_{\text{pre-red}, 0}^{\mu, \nu} := \tilde{\V}_{\mu} \cdot 
    R_\rho(\Phi^\text{stat}_{N'}(\theta^*_\text{sDFNN}))(\mu, \nu) \in \R^{N_A},
\end{equation*}
for any $(\mu, \nu) \in \Theta \times \Theta'$.
Following this, we now implement a CoLoRA neural network architecture, which defines
neural networks in terms of CoLoRA layers.
\begin{definition}[CoLoRA network]
    Let $N_A, L, R \in \N$ with $R \ll N_A$, then a \emph{CoLoRA-layered neural network $\Phi_\alpha$ 
    with input dimensional $N_A$ and $L$ layers} is
    defined via 
    \begin{equation*}
        \Phi_\alpha = ((W_1, A_1, B_1, b_1), \dots, (W_L, A_L, B_L, b_L)),
    \end{equation*}
    such that $N_0 = N_A$ and $N_1, \dots, N_L \in \N$, where $W_l$ is a $(N_l \times \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} N_k)$-, 
    $A_l$ a $(N_l \times R)$-, $B_l$ a $(R \times \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} N_k)$-dimensional matrix, and $b_l \in \R^{N_l}$ for
    each $l = 1, \dots, L$.
    We then denote with
    \begin{equation*}
        R_\rho(\Phi_\alpha): \Theta' \times [0, T] \times \R^{N_A} \to \R^{N_L}
    \end{equation*}
    be a \emph{realization of $\Phi_\alpha$ with activation
    function $\rho$ and latent state $\alpha$} for some $\rho: \R \to \R$ and $\alpha = [\alpha_l]_{l=1}^L$,
    with $\alpha_l: \Theta' \times [0, T] \to \R$ some continuous map for all $l=1, \dots, L$,
    as the result of the following scheme for $\nu \in \Theta', t \in [0, T]$ and $x \in \R^{N_A}$
    \begin{align*}
        x_0 &:= x, \\
        x_l &:= \rho \left( W_l x_{l-1} + \alpha_l(\nu, t)\, A_l B_l x_{l-1} + b_l \right), \quad \mbox{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1, \\
        x_L &:= W_L x_{L-1} + \alpha_L(\nu, t)\, A_L B_L x_{L-1} + b_L.
    \end{align*}
\end{definition}
Finally, with this definition, we can set up the total workflow of the proposed method.
For the latent state $\alpha$, we use a simple realization of an underlying neural network, a DFNN, 
where we set up a fixed layer size for each of them. This strategy is simply lifted from \cite{berman2024},
but as the authors have mentioned there too, this can be replaced by some other continuous mapping.

The approximate solution to the time-dependent problem in Equation (\ref{eq: full discrete ppde}) is given by
the realization of a CoLoRA network $\Phi_\alpha$, and a lift
using the time-trajectory sensitive POD, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
    \hat u_{h}^{\mu, \nu, t} = \A \cdot R_\rho(\Phi_\alpha)(\nu, t, \hat{u}_{\text{pre-red}, 0}^{\mu, \nu}) \in \R^{N_h}.
\end{equation*}
Since, the trainable network of this method is defined again on the span of the $N_A$-dimensional POD
matrix, we can set up the reduced training data as
\begin{equation*}
    \uhtprered := \A^T \G \uht \in \R^{N_A} \quad \text{for all }(\mu, \nu, t) \in \Pcal_1 \times \Pcal_2 \times \Tcal.
\end{equation*}
Let the optimization problem be given for any choice of
neural network weights $\theta = \theta_\alpha \in \Theta(\Phi_\alpha)$ by the loss function
\begin{multline} \label{eq: loss colora}
    \Lcal_\text{CoLoRA}\left(R_\rho(\Phi_\alpha(\theta)) \big\vert \{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t\}_{i,j,k}, 
    \{u_{\text{pre-red}}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} \}_{i,j,k}\right) \\
    = \frac{1}{N_\text{data}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s_1}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{s_2}} 
    \sum_{k = 1}^{N_t} \, \euknorm{u_{\text{pre-red}}^{\mu_i, \nu_j, k \Delta t} - 
    R_\rho(\Phi_\alpha(\theta_\alpha))(\nu_j, k \Delta t, \hat{u}_{\text{pre-red}, 0}^{\mu, \nu})}^2.
\end{multline}


\begin{remark}[Heuristic Discussion]
    The idea of this adapted approach is that many partial differential equations, such as parabolic ones, 
    often have an intrinsically linked stationary limit. Further, specifically parabolic equations tend to gain smoothness
    in growing time. Hence, the continuous adaptation of an initial approximate solution might make sense.
    Additionally, to counter the KnW barrier, we employ the DOD method for a cheap reduction in case of a hybrid parameter
    problem.
\end{remark}
